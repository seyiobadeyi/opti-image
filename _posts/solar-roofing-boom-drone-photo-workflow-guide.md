**Three-quarters of residential roofing contractors and 72% of commercial contractors expect solar sales to grow this year, and solar's share of commercial roofing revenue has already tripled from 1% to 3% since 2024.** That growth is changing what "documenting a job" means for a roofing crew. A standard reroof generates a handful of before-and-after shots from the ground. A solar install generates a full documentation trail — structural condition, panel alignment, wiring, shading exposure — much of which simply isn't visible from a ladder with a phone camera, no matter how good the phone is.

## Why Solar Jobs Need More Than a Phone Camera Can Give

A solar array's performance depends on things a ground-level photo can't show: whether every panel is aligned correctly across the full roof plane, whether a tree or vent pipe is casting partial shade on part of the array at certain times of day, and whether the mounting hardware sits flush and even across a surface that might not be perfectly visible from any single ground vantage point. Phone photos from a ladder capture individual sections at an angle; they don't capture the roof as a whole system, which is exactly what a solar array is.

## The Documentation Load Is the Part Nobody Budgets For

A standard reroof needs a before shot and an after shot, mostly for marketing and a quick client record. A solar install needs a genuinely different documentation set: structural condition photos before any panel goes up (for insurance and liability purposes), mid-install shots showing correct panel spacing and wiring routes (often required by manufacturer warranty terms), and a full overhead post-install set showing the completed array's alignment and any shading obstructions. That's three to four times the photo volume of a standard job, with a higher bar for what counts as adequate documentation, on jobs that are becoming a bigger share of a growing number of contractors' business.

## Building a Workflow That Doesn't Fall Behind

1. **Fly the overhead pass before ground-level shots, not after.** A drone pass showing the full roof plane and surrounding shade sources sets the frame for what the ground-level detail shots need to cover — doing it last means re-shooting when the overhead pass reveals something the ground photos missed.
2. **Shoot mid-install checkpoints, not just before-and-after.** Panel alignment and wiring routes get covered up as the install progresses; a photo taken only at the end can't prove what the wiring looked like before the final panel went on.
3. **Standardize a shot list per solar job, separate from your standard reroof shot list.** Treating a solar install's documentation needs like a bigger version of a normal reroof shoot means someone inevitably forgets a required warranty or insurance angle.
4. **Compress the full set before it goes into a client file or insurance submission.** Drone photos and full-roof aerial shots produce large files fast, and a documentation package that's too large to upload smoothly to an insurance portal or manufacturer warranty system creates its own delay.

## Bundled Jobs Make This Even More Important

More homeowners are combining a roof replacement with a solar install into a single project, and contractors are increasingly bundling with HVAC or other trades to offer a full-service package. That means a single job's photo file now needs to satisfy a roofing warranty, a solar equipment warranty, an insurance record, and marketing content — all from one shoot. A structured drone-plus-ground workflow that gets built once and reused on every solar job is what keeps that from turning into a scramble on every project.
