**Tesla launched driverless Robotaxi service in Miami this week with no safety monitor in the car at all — a first for the program, which now covers five US cities.** The company says it's targeting a dozen states by the end of 2026. Every launch city produces the same wave of social content: people filming their first ride from the back seat, phone pressed to the window, capturing an empty driver's seat and a wheel turning itself. Almost all of that footage looks worse than it should, and the reason has nothing to do with the camera.

## Why In-Car Footage Is Genuinely Hard to Shoot Well

A moving car creates three lighting and glass problems simultaneously, and phone cameras handle none of them gracefully. Tinted windows cut incoming light, which pushes your phone's sensor to boost ISO and introduce noise. The glass itself reflects the interior — your own hand, the dashboard display, a stray light source — directly into the shot. And because the car is moving, your phone's auto-exposure keeps hunting between the bright exterior and dark interior, producing footage that visibly pulses brighter and darker every few seconds. None of that is a "bad phone" problem. It's physics, and it's the same physics every dashcam video and every rideshare passenger video has always fought.

## Shoot It Right the First Time

You don't fix glare and reflections in post-production — you avoid them while filming. This is the order that actually works, in the ten seconds you have before you're already recording anyway:

1. **Wipe the window with your sleeve first.** Fingerprints and interior condensation scatter light more than tint ever will. This is the single biggest improvement and it costs nothing.
2. **Angle your phone slightly, not flat against the glass.** Dead-flat against the window maximizes reflection of the interior. A 10-15 degree angle cuts most of it.
3. **Kill interior lights and screens where you can.** The Tesla's own center display is usually the single brightest reflection source in the shot — angling away from it, or filming from a seat that isn't facing it directly, helps more than any camera setting.
4. **Shoot vertical, not horizontal.** Every platform this footage ends up on — Reels, Shorts, TikTok, Stories — displays vertical natively. Horizontal footage gets cropped or letterboxed on all of them.
5. **Let the exposure settle before you start the clip you actually want.** Tap to lock exposure on the empty driver's seat or the road ahead, not on a bright window, so the footage doesn't hunt for exposure the whole ride.

## The Part Nobody Thinks About Until Upload Fails

Modern phones shoot 4K by default, and a two-minute Robotaxi ride at 4K/60fps can easily land at 400-600MB. That's fine for local storage. It's not fine for a group chat, and it's not fine for a platform upload that stalls at 40% on a spotty connection outside a downtown high-rise. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Why does my Robotaxi video look darker than what I actually saw?**
A: Tinted glass cuts real light reaching your phone's sensor, and the car's own auto-exposure is fighting the bright exterior visible through the windows at the same time. Locking exposure on the interior before you start filming fixes most of it.

**Q: Should I film horizontal or vertical for social media?**
A: Vertical, unless you're specifically making something for YouTube long-form. Every short-form platform — Reels, Shorts, TikTok — displays vertical natively and will crop or pad horizontal footage.

**Q: My video won't upload or takes forever to send — why?**
A: Default 4K recording produces files in the hundreds of megabytes for even a short clip. Compress before sending; a properly compressed clip uploads in seconds instead of stalling out on a weak connection.
