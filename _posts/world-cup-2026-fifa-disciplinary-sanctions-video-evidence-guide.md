**FIFA's disciplinary rulings from the World Cup final's post-match scuffle came down this August, more than a month after the July 19 final itself — and every charge in the case traces back to camera footage, not eyewitness accounts.** Leandro Paredes drew the harshest sanction, a 10-match suspension and $90,000 fine, with Nahuel Molina banned 7 matches, Roberto Ayala 3, and Thiago Almada 1. The case is a clean example of how thoroughly video evidence now drives sports discipline at the highest level — and a useful reminder for anyone sharing a "did you see this happen" sports clip that a single angle rarely tells the whole story.

## What Actually Happened, and What FIFA Charged

Spain beat Argentina 1-0 in extra time on July 19 to win its second World Cup title, and the tension boiled over in the moments immediately after the final whistle. FIFA's disciplinary file, opened July 29 and resolved this month, charged Paredes with three separate counts of assault, Molina with two, and assistant coach Ayala with one — physical contact charges, not simply unsporting conduct. Molina and Almada also picked up separate charges for unsporting behavior, and Spain's Gavi was charged under the same clause, showing the incident wasn't treated as one-sided even though Argentina's players drew the heavier sanctions.

Separately, Argentina's federation was charged over a political banner referencing the Falkland Islands displayed after the semifinal win over England, and over discriminatory chants from supporters during the tournament — a reminder that FIFA disciplinary cases at a World Cup cover far more than on-field contact, and nearly all of it gets built on recorded evidence rather than match-day incident reports alone.

## Why This Is a Video Evidence Story, Not Just a Discipline Story

What separates a modern FIFA disciplinary case from one twenty years ago is the sheer density of recorded angles available for review. A final at this scale is covered by dozens of broadcast cameras, plus goal-line and VAR-dedicated feeds, plus tens of thousands of fans in the stadium with phones — any one of whom might have captured an angle the broadcast missed. Building a case that assigns three separate assault charges to one player and two to another isn't possible from a single wide shot; it requires reconciling multiple recordings, confirming they show the same moment from different angles, and sequencing exactly who made contact with whom and when.

This is functionally the same discipline that photo and video verification does in journalism and sports reporting more broadly — checking that a piece of visual evidence is what it claims to be before treating it as fact. A clip circulating on social media claiming to show "the real story" of an incident is worth exactly as much as its metadata and context support. Timestamp, camera angle, and whether the clip has been cropped or slowed in a way that changes what it appears to show all matter before drawing a conclusion from it — which is precisely the standard FIFA's own disciplinary panel had to apply here across a much larger body of footage than any single fan clip.

## What This Means If You're Archiving Your Own Match Footage

If you shot or screen-recorded footage from the final or any World Cup match this summer, this case is a good reason to keep your originals rather than only the compressed version you already shared. Original files retain metadata — timestamp, and often location — that gets stripped the moment a clip is re-compressed and reshared through social media. If you ever want to reference exactly when you captured something, or contribute footage to a record of an event, the unedited original is the version that actually holds evidentiary value.

1. **Keep an unedited original of anything you filmed at a major sporting event**, separate from the compressed copy you post.
2. **Don't strip metadata from your own archive copies** — save that step for versions you're posting publicly. [Optimage's metadata tool](/metadata) is for the public copy, not your personal archive.
3. **Label files with the actual date and rough time**, since phone folder names rarely preserve that clearly once files get moved or backed up elsewhere.
