**Roughly half of US households now have some form of water-saving plumbing fixture installed, and a big share of that adoption is being driven directly by utility rebate programs paying homeowners to make the switch.** That's good news for water conservation and good repeat business for plumbers. It also means an increasing share of a plumbing company's invoicing now depends on a step that has nothing to do with the actual plumbing work: getting a photo of the install that a rebate reviewer will actually accept.

A rebate reviewer isn't in the building, so a photo has to do the job an in-person inspection used to do: prove the specific qualifying model was actually installed, not just that some fixture was swapped in. That means a wide, well-lit shot of a toilet or faucet from three feet away doesn't cut it if the model or serial plate isn't independently legible. It's a genuinely different photo standard than "documenting the job for my own records," and it's the single most common reason a rebate claim bounces back for resubmission — which delays the homeowner's payment and puts the plumber back in the awkward position of asking a customer for a second visit or a follow-up photo.

## What Actually Clears Rebate Review

1. **A close, sharp shot of the model or serial plate specifically** — not as part of a wider shot, as its own separate photo, taken close enough that the text is unambiguous even after the platform you're sending it through compresses it further.
2. **The water-supply connection**, confirming the fixture is actually plumbed in and not just sitting in place for the photo.
3. **A wide "after" shot of the finished install** in context — the bathroom or kitchen it's installed in — which most programs want alongside the close-up, not instead of it.
4. **A "before" photo of the old fixture**, taken before removal, for programs that require proof of what was actually replaced.

## The Compression Trap That Causes Most Rejections

Here's the part plumbers run into constantly without realizing it's the actual cause: a serial plate that's perfectly legible on the phone screen right after the photo is taken can become blurry and unreadable by the time it reaches a rebate reviewer's inbox, because the messaging app used to send it — texting the office, emailing a customer, uploading through a portal — recompressed the image and degraded exactly the fine text detail that mattered most. The fix isn't shooting closer, necessarily. It's controlling the compression yourself before the photo goes anywhere, instead of letting whatever app it passes through decide how much detail survives.

[Optimage's compressor](/compress) lets you control file size and quality directly, so the model plate stays legible in the file you actually submit — free, in the browser, before a messaging app's automatic compression does it for you and gets it wrong.

## What to Build Into Every Install From Now On

Make the four-photo set — serial plate close-up, supply connection, wide after shot, before shot — a standard last step on every water-saving fixture job, the same way a final pressure test already is. It costs thirty seconds per job and it's the difference between a rebate claim that clears on the first submission and one that bounces back three weeks later with a customer asking why their money hasn't shown up yet.
