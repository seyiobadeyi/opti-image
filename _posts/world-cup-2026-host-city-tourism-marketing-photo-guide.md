**Sixteen cities across the United States, Mexico, and Canada just spent roughly a month as the backdrop for the most-watched sporting event on the planet, generating a volume of drone footage, broadcast b-roll, and fan photography that would cost any single tourism board a fortune to commission independently.** Four days past the final, most of that content is already scattering into personal camera rolls, broadcaster archives, and social feeds that will never be systematically gathered — which is a genuinely wasted opportunity for the destination marketing organizations that spent years angling for host status in the first place.

A single well-produced tourism ad campaign with drone footage and international reach costs a mid-size destination marketing budget real money and takes months to produce. The World Cup delivered something functionally similar — sweeping aerial stadium shots, skyline establishing frames, street-level fan energy — for free, generated by broadcasters and fans rather than commissioned by the tourism board at all. The cities that treat that as a raw asset to be gathered, licensed, and repurposed get years of marketing value out of a month they didn't have to pay to produce. The cities that don't get nothing but the memory of having hosted.

## Two Very Different Asset Types, Two Different Playbooks

Fan photography and broadcast-quality footage aren't the same asset and shouldn't be handled the same way. Fan content — the thousands of phone photos and short clips tagged with a stadium's geolocation or a host city's hashtag — is high-volume and low-cost to gather but comes with real usage-rights questions; using someone's photo in an official campaign generally requires their permission, not just a public post. Broadcast and official FIFA or host-broadcaster footage is the opposite: fewer assets, but licensable at a defined cost and cleared for genuinely high-production use, assuming a tourism board moves before licensing windows close.

1. **Fan content**: build a permission-request pipeline now, while the tournament is still fresh in people's minds and they're more likely to respond to a request to feature their photo.
2. **Broadcast content**: contact FIFA's or the relevant host broadcaster's licensing office promptly — tournament footage rights and windows are typically time-limited, and waiting months to start the conversation risks missing the usable window entirely.

Once a tourism board actually has the content gathered and rights cleared, the biggest practical failure point is treating it as one campaign instead of a multi-year archive. A batch of stadium and skyline photos compressed and standardized once can be redeployed across seasonal campaigns, convention bids, and travel partner materials for years — the tournament created the content, but its marketing life doesn't have to end when the news cycle does.

1. **Standardize resolution and format across the whole gathered archive** so assets from a phone camera, a drone operator, and a broadcaster's b-roll all sit at a consistent quality level in the same content library.
2. **Compress for the actual channel each piece will run on** — a billboard-bound photo and a social post need very different file handling from the same source image.
3. **Tag and organize by neighborhood, venue, and season** now, while the context is fresh, so the archive is actually searchable two years from now instead of a dumped folder nobody can navigate.

## What to Take From This

The World Cup handed sixteen host cities a marketing archive most destinations spend years and real budget trying to build, and the value of it doesn't expire when the tournament coverage does — it expires when nobody bothers to gather, clear, and organize it before it scatters. The tourism boards that treat this month as raw material rather than a closed chapter will be running campaigns off it well into 2028.
