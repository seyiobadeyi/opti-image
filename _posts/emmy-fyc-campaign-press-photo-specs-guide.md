**HBO Max's The Pitt leads the 78th Primetime Emmy nominations with 25, ahead of Hacks with 24, heading into a September 14 ceremony hosted by Mariska Hargitay.** The Creative Arts Emmys — honoring the technical and craft categories — air just before that, on September 5 and 6. Between nominations landing and votes closing, every studio and network runs a For-Your-Consideration campaign, and a large piece of that campaign is a press image library most viewers never think about but that shapes almost everything written about a nominated show in the run-up to the ceremony.

## What an FYC Photo Push Actually Involves

When a show picks up serious nomination momentum — the kind The Pitt and Hacks are riding into this ceremony — its studio's publicity team builds out a press image library specifically for trade and entertainment journalists to draw from. This isn't the same as a show's general promotional stills. FYC-specific imagery is often shot separately: cast portraits framed around the specific nominated performance, behind-the-scenes production stills that support "For Your Consideration" trade ads, and high-resolution character shots sized for full-page trade magazine placements as much as for web use.

The reason this matters operationally is that every trade outlet has its own preferred specs. Variety wants a different crop and resolution than Deadline; a print trade ad has entirely different requirements than a web gallery. A single campaign photo shoot routinely gets exported into half a dozen different versions before it's ever distributed, and getting that turnaround wrong — sending outlets a low-res or wrongly cropped image — can mean a show's coverage runs with a less flattering wire photo instead of the campaign's intended image.

## Craft Categories Add a Layer Most People Never Consider

The Creative Arts Emmys, airing September 5 and 6 ahead of the main ceremony, cover the technical and craft categories — cinematography, editing, production design, visual effects, and dozens of others that rarely get red carpet coverage but still generate their own FYC campaigns. A cinematography nominee's campaign imagery looks fundamentally different from an acting nominee's: it needs to showcase specific frames or techniques from the show itself rather than portrait photography of a performer, and it's often built in close coordination with the show's actual director of photography rather than a separate campaign photographer. A studio running a full-spectrum Emmy campaign across both ceremonies is effectively managing several distinct image libraries simultaneously, each with its own visual logic and its own audience of voters within the Television Academy.

## Why Two Shows at 25 and 24 Nominations Creates Real Competition for Coverage

When two shows are running this close in nomination count — The Pitt's 25 against Hacks's 24 — trade press coverage becomes genuinely competitive for column space and gallery placement in the weeks before voting closes. Both campaigns are pushing image assets to the same limited set of entertainment journalists during the same compressed window, which raises the practical stakes on turnaround speed: a campaign that can fulfill an outlet's image request same-day, in the exact specs that outlet needs, has a real edge over one that takes two days to get back with a wrongly-cropped file. That's a genuinely different kind of urgency than a campaign running unopposed for attention in its category, and it's part of why close nomination races tend to produce noticeably more polished FYC image operations than a more predictable year.

## Streaming Complicates the Old Playbook Further

The FYC campaign playbook was built in an era when most nominated shows aired on a handful of broadcast and cable networks with established relationships to trade press. A streaming-heavy nomination slate like this year's spreads campaign coordination across more platforms, each with its own internal marketing team, its own image approval chain, and its own relationship (or lack of one) with traditional entertainment trade outlets. That fragmentation is part of why FYC image libraries have gotten more complex to manage in recent years, not less — a studio's central publicity team increasingly has to coordinate with a streaming platform's own marketing operation rather than controlling the entire image pipeline in-house the way a traditional network campaign once could.

## How a Well-Run Campaign Image Library Is Built

1. **Shoot in a single high-resolution master format** and export down from there for each outlet, rather than reshooting or re-scanning for every different request that comes in.
2. **Build a specs sheet per major outlet in advance** — resolution, crop ratio, color profile — so image requests during the busiest weeks of the campaign get a same-day turnaround instead of a back-and-forth.
3. **Compress the web-distribution versions without touching the archival masters.** Trade press downloads need to load fast on a journalist's deadline; the original high-res files stay untouched for print and future reuse.
4. **Keep the library organized by nominee and category**, not just by show, since individual acting and craft nominees often need their own dedicated image sets separate from the ensemble cast photos.

## Why This Library Outlives the Ceremony

A well-built FYC image library doesn't get thrown away after the trophies are handed out. The same photo assets get reused in a show's next-season marketing, streaming platform promotional placements, and future "Emmy-winning" branding on posters and thumbnails for years afterward. Treating the campaign photo shoot as a one-off expense misses that it's actually building a reusable asset library — which is one more reason the export and organization step matters as much as the shoot itself.
