**The Clippers and Raptors agreed on June 30 to send Kawhi Leonard back to Toronto in exchange for Brandon Ingram, Gradey Dick, and a stack of future draft capital — and the deal still hasn't closed, because the NBA is investigating whether the Clippers circumvented the salary cap.** There's no announced timetable for when that investigation wraps. What that means in practice, beyond the obvious roster uncertainty, is that both franchises' marketing departments have been sitting for weeks on a full set of trade-day photo assets they can't publish and can't fully abandon either.

## A Trade That's Real on Paper, Frozen Everywhere Else

Front offices don't wait for league approval to start preparing the announcement. The moment two teams agree to terms, someone on each marketing staff is already mocking up the new jersey number, building the "Welcome to Toronto" graphic, and blocking time for a press-day photo shoot. That work typically happens in the days immediately following a handshake agreement — not after formal approval, which is usually treated as a formality that clears within a week or two.

This deal broke that pattern. Leonard agreed to terms with Toronto nearly a month ago, and the investigation has kept the trade from becoming official since. Both teams are now in an unusual holding position: Toronto has assets ready to promote a star who isn't officially theirs, and the Clippers have assets acknowledging a departure that, technically, hasn't happened yet.

## Why This Isn't Just a Front-Office Problem

Every player switching teams in this deal — not just Leonard — has assets stuck in the same limbo. Brandon Ingram and Gradey Dick, headed to Los Angeles, have Clippers-branded graphics prepared and unpublished the same way Leonard has Raptors ones. That's a meaningful volume of finished creative work sitting in shared drives, waiting on a legal outcome nobody controls the timing of.

The practical risk isn't that the photos get lost — it's the opposite. A held asset like this tends to get shared internally more than a published one would, forwarded between departments for sign-off, previewed by people outside the original creative team, occasionally leaked a version early by accident. Every one of those internal shares is a chance for an unpublished, unapproved graphic to end up outside the building before the trade — or the investigation's outcome — is public.

## What Actually Happens to Assets in This Kind of Holding Pattern

1. **Version control gets harder the longer the hold lasts.** A month-old jersey mockup needs to be checked against the current roster numbers and any depth-chart changes before it's safe to publish, not assumed to still be accurate.
2. **File formats matter more for something sitting unused.** A high-resolution source file compressed once for internal review and then compressed again for final publish loses more visible quality than compressing directly from the original.
3. **Access needs tightening, not loosening, during a hold.** The instinct is often to relax controls on an asset that "isn't going anywhere," when the opposite is true — a held asset benefits from fewer hands and fewer copies floating around, not more.
4. **A held asset should be treated as still in production**, not archived, so it stays organized under the same naming and folder conventions actively-worked files use rather than getting buried in a "pending" folder nobody checks.

## What to Take From This

A trade this size doesn't pause the creative work behind it — it just pauses the button that publishes it. Both franchises are sitting on finished promotional material for a deal that could close on a week's notice or drag for months, and the smart move for anyone managing those assets is treating them as live, version-controlled files rather than something to set aside until the league makes a call.
