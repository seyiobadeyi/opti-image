**Wimbledon photographers spend two weeks shooting the same light: overcast-to-bright English daylight on grass, start to finish, every match.** The hard-court swing that leads into the US Open doesn't offer that consistency. Events like the DC Open running through early August put matches on court starting in afternoon sun and finishing under stadium lights after dark — sometimes both in the same match if a three-setter runs long. That's a lighting condition Wimbledon's grass-court, daylight-only schedule simply doesn't create, and it changes how a photographer has to shoot.

## Two Different Photography Problems, Two Different Surfaces

Grass and hard courts don't just play differently — they photograph differently. Grass is a light-absorbing surface with a muted, consistent tone that doesn't fight a photographer's exposure reading. Hard courts, particularly the blue-and-green acrylic combination standard across the US Open series, are much more reflective. Light bounces up off the court surface into the frame from below as well as arriving from above, which changes what a camera's meter sees compared to the same lens and settings used on grass weeks earlier.

Add day-into-night sessions, which the grass-court calendar mostly avoids by scheduling around natural light, and a hard-court event photographer is dealing with two variables Wimbledon coverage doesn't: a more reflective surface and a light source that can change entirely mid-match.

## Why This Trips Up Photographers Coming Straight Off Grass

The US hard-court swing runs directly on the calendar's heels of the grass season, which means a lot of photographers are covering both within a few weeks of each other. Muscle memory built shooting two weeks of consistent grass-court daylight doesn't transfer cleanly to a hard-court event where the same court can be lit by sun at 2pm and stadium floodlights by 9pm. A white balance setting dialed in for daylight will render everything shot under floodlights with a color cast that needs correcting — and if a match runs across both conditions, no single in-camera setting covers the whole sequence.

## What Actually Works on the Hard-Court Circuit

1. **Shoot RAW, not JPEG, for anything on a day-into-night session.** RAW preserves the sensor data needed to correct white balance accurately after the fact; a JPEG's baked-in white balance from a sunlit first set can't be cleanly fixed once the match moves under lights.
2. **Treat sunlight and stadium-light portions of a match as two separate editing passes.** Don't try to apply one color grade across an entire match sequence that changed lighting conditions partway through — split the edit at the point the light changed.
3. **Meter off the player, not the court.** The reflective hard-court surface can fool an all-scene meter reading into underexposing a player's face and kit; spot-metering on the subject compensates for the extra bounced light from below.
4. **Re-check white balance at every session change, not just once per day.** A photographer covering back-to-back day and night sessions across multiple courts needs to reset for each transition, not assume the morning's calibration holds all day.

## The Last Step Nobody Skips on Deadline

Whichever surface a tournament is shot on, the file coming out of a corrected RAW edit is large — often larger than a straight-from-camera JPEG, since a proper white balance and exposure correction pass tends to be done on the highest-quality export available. A photographer or stringer filing from courtside on a tournament Wi-Fi connection doesn't have time to wrestle with an oversized file mid-upload. [Compressing the finished edit](/compress) before transmission keeps quality intact while cutting the file down to something that actually uploads fast on a shared media-center connection, instead of timing out on deadline because the file was three times larger than it needed to be.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why is hard-court tennis photography harder to light correctly than grass-court photography?**
Grass courts at tournaments like Wimbledon are shot almost entirely in consistent daylight. Hard-court swing events run day sessions into night sessions on the same court, meaning a photographer can start a match under sunlight and finish it under stadium floodlights, with the color temperature shifting mid-match.

**Does court color affect tennis photo exposure?**
Yes. Hard courts, especially the blue and green acrylic surfaces common on the US hard-court circuit, reflect significantly more ambient light upward than grass does, which changes how a camera's light meter reads the scene and can produce different exposure results from the same settings used on grass.

## Summary

- Grass-court tournaments are shot in one consistent daylight condition; the hard-court swing into the US Open mixes day and night sessions in the same match
- Reflective hard-court surfaces change exposure metering compared to grass, on top of the lighting-condition shift
- Shooting RAW and correcting white balance per lighting change, rather than per match, is what actually holds up on a day-into-night session
- Once the edit is done, [compress the final files](/compress) before filing — a corrected RAW export is heavy, and courtside Wi-Fi isn't built for that
