**A week out from Spain's extra-time win over Argentina in the final, the physical souvenirs from this World Cup — ticket stubs, jerseys, scarves, wristbands — are already on the same slow fade timeline every past tournament's memorabilia went through.** Thermal-printed tickets in particular start visibly degrading within a few years of normal handling. The fix isn't a display case, though those help. It's photographing the objects properly now, while they're still sharp, so the digital record outlasts whatever happens to the physical one.

## Why the Physical Copy Is a Bad Long-Term Bet

Most modern tickets — sports, concerts, anything printed at a kiosk or scanned at a gate — use thermal printing rather than ink. That process relies on a heat-sensitive coating, and the same property that lets a printer produce it instantly is what makes it fade: exposure to light, warmth, or even the friction of being handled and stored in a wallet degrades that coating steadily. A World Cup ticket stub kept loose in a drawer can be noticeably faded within two or three years, sometimes less.

Jerseys and scarves hold up better as physical objects, but they're not immune either — fabric fades, printed numbers crack, and a jersey worn for photos repeatedly picks up wear that a purely archival copy wouldn't. None of that is a reason not to keep the physical items. It's a reason not to rely on them as the only record.

## How to Actually Photograph a Ticket Stub

A ticket held at an angle to catch good light usually introduces the opposite problem — glare across the printed text, or a shadow from your own hand crossing exactly the part you're trying to preserve. The better approach is closer to how a document scanner works than how you'd normally take a photo:

1. **Lay the stub flat on a plain, matte, non-reflective surface** — a dark or neutral-colored desk works better than anything glossy.
2. **Shoot directly overhead**, not at an angle, using your phone's grid lines to keep the camera parallel to the surface.
3. **Use even, diffuse light** — daylight near a window without direct sun works better than a single overhead bulb, which tends to create hot spots and shadow gradients across small text.
4. **Capture at your camera's highest resolution setting**, since thermal print detail is small and a downscaled capture loses legibility fast.

## How to Photograph Jerseys and Scarves for an Archive, Not Just a Photo

A worn jersey photo is a great personal keepsake, but it's a poor archival record — folds, wrinkles, and body shape obscure the actual printed detail, numbers, and stitching. For the archival version specifically, lay the item flat against a neutral background, smooth out major creases, and shoot straight down from directly above. That flat, evenly lit shot is what actually holds up as a reference years later, separate from whatever more casual photos you take wearing it.

## Building the Archive So It Actually Lasts

1. **Keep an uncompressed or high-quality master copy** of every scan and photo, stored somewhere with a backup — this is the file you'll be glad exists in ten years.
2. **Create a separate, compressed version for anything you actually share or post**, rather than uploading the master and letting a platform's own compression handle it inconsistently.
3. **Name and organize files by match or item**, not by date taken — "final-ticket-stub" ages better as a searchable label than a timestamp you'll forget the context of.
4. **Convert everything to a widely-supported format** like JPEG or PNG rather than leaving files in a phone-specific format that may not open cleanly on whatever device you're using in a decade.

## What to Do This Week

If you were at a match, or picked up a jersey or scarf from this tournament, the window to get a clean scan is now, not "eventually" — thermal ink doesn't wait for you to get around to it. A flat surface, overhead light, and ten minutes per item is the whole project, and it's the difference between a memory you can still read clearly in 2036 and a faded gray rectangle in a drawer.
