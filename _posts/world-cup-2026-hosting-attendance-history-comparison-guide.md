**Tomorrow's final closes out a tournament that will have drawn more total fans through stadium gates than Qatar 2022 and Russia 2018 combined, and that's mostly a function of format, not popularity.** The jump from 32 teams to 48, and from one host country to three, mechanically produces more matches, more stadiums, and more total attendance than either of the last two tournaments could have generated regardless of how compelling the football itself was. Worth understanding the actual shape of that comparison before the final tomorrow closes the book on this one.

## Why the Comparison Isn't Really About Popularity

Qatar 2022 ran 64 matches across eight stadiums in a single small country, all within a tight geographic radius that let fans attend multiple matches in the same week without much travel. That compactness produced consistently high per-match attendance in mostly newer, purpose-built stadiums, but capped the tournament's total ceiling at 64 matches no matter how much demand existed.

Russia 2018 also ran the 32-team, 64-match format, but across a far larger country with matches spread from Kaliningrad to Yekaterinburg — a genuinely different travel and hosting challenge from Qatar's tight footprint, even though the match count was identical. Comparing "total attendance" across 2018 and 2022 mostly comes down to stadium capacity choices and how many neutral fans could feasibly travel between host cities, not underlying interest in the tournament.

2026 changes the math structurally. Forty-eight teams means 104 matches instead of 64 — a 62% increase in the raw number of games before a single ticket consideration enters the picture. Three host countries (with matches spread across the US, Mexico, and Canada) means a wider range of stadium capacities, including several NFL stadiums with considerably higher capacity than most dedicated soccer venues used in 2022. More matches, more stadiums, bigger average stadium size — the total attendance record this tournament is on pace to set was close to guaranteed by the format decision made years before a ball was kicked.

## If You've Photographed More Than One of These

If you're a fan who's personally attended or closely followed multiple World Cups — 2018, 2022, and now this one — you're sitting on a photo archive that spans close to a decade, three very different hosting formats, and almost certainly three very different phones with three different default camera settings. That's a genuinely harder organizational problem than it sounds, because each tournament's photos tend to live wherever they were originally saved: an old phone's backup, a cloud account from years ago, a folder nobody's opened since 2022.

Building a single unified archive — same folder structure, same naming convention (tournament year, stage, opponent), same compression standard applied retroactively to the older files — is what actually makes it possible to look back across tournaments rather than just accumulating separate, incompatible photo dumps. It's also the only way a "then and now" comparison photo actually works cleanly, since old and new files at wildly different original resolutions and quality levels don't sit well side by side without some normalization first.

## Practical Steps for Building a Cross-Tournament Archive

1. **Consolidate photos from every World Cup you've followed into one shared structure**, organized by year and stage rather than left scattered across devices.
2. **Normalize resolution and compression across the whole archive**, so a 2018 photo and a 2026 photo don't look jarringly mismatched side by side.
3. **Tag or caption by match and stage while you still remember the details** — this is the step everyone skips and regrets a tournament or two later.
4. **Build the gallery now, not four years from now** — waiting until the next World Cup to organize this one's photos means doing it for two tournaments' worth of files instead of one.
