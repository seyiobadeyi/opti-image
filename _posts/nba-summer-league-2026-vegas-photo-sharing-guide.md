Neither stat line settles anything long-term, but the box score from a Tuesday night in July isn't really the point. The point is that this rivalry now has a professional chapter, and thousands of people in that building tried to capture it on their phones.

## Why Summer League Photos Look Worse Than the Regular Season

Every NBA fan who's been to a Vegas Summer League game notices the same thing without knowing why: the photos come out worse than the ones they take at a normal arena game. It's not the crowd, it's not the seats, it's the light. Summer League is played in practice-facility-style gyms and secondary arenas that mix overhead LED panels with broadcast lighting rigs set up for the TV cameras, and those two sources rarely agree on color temperature. A phone's auto white balance is built to average out one dominant light source, not referee a fight between two. The result is the slightly greenish or orange cast you see in almost every Summer League phone photo posted to social media that week.

On top of that, the pace of play pushes your phone's ISO up automatically to freeze motion in a dimmer room than a regular-season arena, and higher ISO means more visible grain — the same reason a photo taken in a dim restaurant looks noisier than one taken outside at noon. None of that is something you fix by buying a better phone. It's a lighting and physics problem, and the camera app is already doing the best it reasonably can in real time.

## What Actually Helps After the Fact

You can't undo sensor noise that's already baked into a shot, but you can stop making it worse before you post it.

1. **Shoot at the resolution the moment deserves, not zoomed in.** Digital zoom on a phone at a distance from the court just crops and upscales a smaller number of real pixels — it doesn't get you closer. A wider shot cropped afterward on a real screen, at real resolution, almost always looks sharper than one zoomed in at capture time.
2. **Don't let the platform's auto-compression be the last thing that touches the file.** Instagram, X, and group chats all re-encode whatever you upload, and a shot that was already noisy gets blockier on top of that. Compress and resize it yourself first, at a size and quality you control, so the platform has less damage to do.
3. **Fix the color cast before you post, not after someone points it out.** A quick white balance correction on a mixed-lighting shot does more for how "good" a photo looks than any amount of sharpening. Warm, greenish Summer League photos read as low-effort even when the moment itself was worth capturing.
