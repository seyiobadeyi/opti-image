**Renters have unusual leverage right now — new 2026 laws in California cap security deposits at one month's rent, Colorado now treats deposits as tenant property held in trust, and advisors are telling tenants to start renewal conversations 90 to 120 days out to negotiate from strength.** All of that leverage is worth nothing in an actual dispute without photo evidence. Landlords and tenants remember "the condition of the apartment" very differently once money is on the line, and the only thing that settles the argument is a timestamped photo showing who's right.

## Why This Is a Bigger Deal in 2026 Specifically

Security deposit law changed meaningfully this year in several states. Illinois now requires landlords to hand over a summary of tenant protections on the first page of every lease. Colorado's new trust-property rule means a landlord who mishandles a deposit is on much shakier legal ground than before. California's one-month cap means less money is at stake per unit, but that money is more contested precisely because it's a smaller, more defined pool. None of these laws enforce themselves — they shift the burden of proof, and the tenant who shows up with dated photos wins that shift. The tenant who shows up with nothing is back to a he-said-she-said argument regardless of what the law says.

## The Five Photos to Take

1. **Every wall in every room, straight-on, in good light.** Not artistic angles — flat, boring, well-lit shots that clearly show paint condition, any existing marks, and any damage. This is the photo that stops a landlord from charging you for a scuff that was already there.
2. **All flooring, including under furniture where practical.** Carpet stains and scratched hardwood are two of the most commonly disputed charges. A wide shot of the full floor plus close-ups of any existing wear covers you on both counts.
3. **Every appliance, inside and out.** Open the oven, the fridge, the dishwasher. A landlord charging a cleaning fee for a stove that was already dirty when you moved in has no case against a dated photo showing exactly that.
4. **Fixtures and hardware — faucets, cabinet handles, light fixtures, blinds.** Small stuff, but small stuff is exactly what shows up on itemized deduction lists. Photograph it now so there's no argument about when it broke.
5. **A wide shot of each full room from the doorway.** This is your overall-condition reference — the shot that shows the room as a whole, in context, rather than just isolated problem spots.

## Keep the Original File, Not a Forward

This is the step most people skip and it undermines everything above. If you take your move-in photos, then text them to a friend or family member "just in case," and later only have that forwarded copy, you've likely lost the timestamp — most messaging apps strip EXIF metadata on send, including the date and time the photo was actually taken. In a dispute, a photo with intact metadata proving when it was shot is worth far more than the same image with no way to verify its date.

Keep your originals in a dedicated folder, back them up to cloud storage that preserves metadata, and only compress or crop copies you're sharing — never the source files you might need as evidence a year from now. If storage space is tight because you're not used to keeping twenty or thirty full-resolution photos around, [compress a duplicate set](/compress) for everyday viewing and keep the untouched originals archived separately.

- New 2026 laws in California, Colorado, and Illinois give renters real leverage — but only tenants who can prove condition benefit from it

- Store the untouched original files, not forwarded or screenshotted copies, since messaging apps typically strip the timestamp that makes a photo useful as evidence
