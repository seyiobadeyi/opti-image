**Dermatology telehealth visits climb every summer as sun exposure pushes more people to finally check that spot they've been meaning to look at, and a large share of the photos submitted for those remote visits are too compressed to actually be useful.** This isn't a rare edge case — it's the default outcome of sending a photo through a messaging app or email client tuned to shrink file size, not preserve the specific visual detail a dermatologist is trying to assess.

## Why This Season Specifically

More sun exposure means more visible skin, more time spent outdoors noticing changes, and more people prompted to finally act on a skin concern they'd been putting off through the colder months. Clinics and telehealth platforms see this pattern reliably every year — a predictable summer bump in both new-patient skin checks and existing-patient follow-ups on spots being monitored over time.

Messaging apps and email clients apply aggressive automatic compression tuned to keep attachments small and load fast — a reasonable default for a vacation photo, a genuinely poor default for a close-up meant to show subtle texture and border irregularity. The specific visual features a dermatologist is trained to assess — asymmetry, border definition, color variation, fine surface texture — are exactly the kind of high-frequency detail that aggressive compression smooths away first, well before the photo looks obviously degraded to an untrained eye.

The result is a photo that looks fine to the patient sending it and noticeably inadequate to the clinician receiving it, which either forces a request for a better photo (delaying the assessment) or, worse, an assessment made on genuinely insufficient visual information.

## What Actually Makes a Diagnostic Photo Useful

1. **Shoot in even, natural light** — direct overhead sun or harsh indoor lighting creates shadows that distort how a spot's borders and texture actually read.
2. **Get close enough that the area of concern fills a meaningful portion of the frame**, without losing focus — most phone cameras need a beat to autofocus at close range; wait for it before capturing.
3. **Include a size reference**, like a coin, placed near the area. Clinicians consistently cite the lack of a scale reference as one of the most common reasons a submitted photo is hard to assess.
4. **Export and send at full resolution**, not through a messaging app's default compression. If file size is a genuine constraint for the clinic's intake system, use a controlled compression pass rather than whatever a chat app defaults to.
5. **Take a follow-up photo from the same distance and angle weeks later** if a spot is being monitored over time — inconsistent framing makes a "has this changed" comparison much harder to judge visually.

## What Clinics Can Do on Their End

Publishing clear, specific photo submission guidelines — lighting, distance, scale reference, file format — before a telehealth visit measurably reduces the number of unusable submissions a clinic has to send back for a retake. Pairing that guidance with an intake pipeline that compresses images to a controlled, diagnostic-quality-preserving standard (rather than whatever compression a patient's device or messaging app happened to apply) closes the loop from both directions.
