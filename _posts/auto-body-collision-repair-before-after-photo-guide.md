A body shop's before/after photos do three jobs that have nothing to do with each other: they support an insurance claim, they protect the shop from a liability dispute, and they sell the next estimate. Most shops shoot one set of photos and hope it covers all three — and it usually doesn't, because a photo good enough for a Facebook post isn't automatically good enough for an adjuster, and a photo good enough for a claim file isn't automatically framed the way a prospective customer needs to see it. The fix isn't three separate photo shoots. It's one disciplined shot list, applied consistently, with the file handling cleaned up on the back end.

## Three Jobs, One Set of Photos

Treat each purpose as a different reader looking at the same image.

**Insurance adjusters** need photos that read as credible evidence, not marketing. That means even lighting, no filters, no cropping that hides context, and enough angles that the adjuster can independently verify the damage matches the estimate. Adjusters see thousands of photo sets a year — anything that looks staged, over-edited, or incomplete triggers a request for more photos, which adds days to a claim.

**Liability protection** is about the record you have, not the photos you post. If a customer disputes a charge or claims you damaged something that was already there, your intake photos are the only thing standing between you and a he-said-she-said argument. This set doesn't need to be pretty. It needs to be complete and timestamped.

**Marketing** photos are the only ones where framing and visual impact matter more than completeness. A dramatic before/after pair on Google Business Profile or your website is what turns a browsing homeowner into a booked estimate. This is the one purpose where a slightly flattering angle is a feature, not a liability risk — as long as the underlying repair documentation still exists separately.

The mistake most shops make is trying to shoot one purpose and reuse it for the other two. A tight, dramatic marketing shot of a repaired quarter panel doesn't hold up as claim evidence because it doesn't show the surrounding context an adjuster needs. A flat, evidentiary intake photo doesn't convert anyone browsing Instagram. Shoot for all three at intake, and you never have to go back for a re-shoot.

## The Standard Multi-Angle Shot List

This is the list to run on every repair order, before a single tool touches the vehicle. It takes under five minutes and covers insurance, liability, and marketing at once.

1. **Four corner shots** — front-left, front-right, rear-left, rear-right, each capturing the full vehicle from about 10 feet back. These establish the vehicle's overall condition and give an adjuster context for where the damage sits relative to the whole car.
2. **Wide shot of the damage area** — back up enough to show the damaged panel plus the panels on either side of it, so viewers can judge scale and confirm nothing adjacent was affected.
3. **Close-up of the damage itself** — fill the frame with the actual dent, scrape, or break. This is the shot an adjuster uses to verify the specific repair line item.
4. **VIN plate** — dashboard or door jamb, whichever is legible. Non-negotiable for claim files and for your own records if a dispute comes up later.
5. **Odometer reading** — ties the photo set to a specific point in the vehicle's history, which matters if the same customer comes back for a second, unrelated repair.
6. **Interior condition, if the customer flags anything** — a quick shot of the seats and dash covers you if a customer later claims something was damaged during the repair that had nothing to do with the body work.
7. **Repeat the same corner and close-up angles post-repair** — matching angles are what make the before/after pair usable for marketing, and matching angles are also what make it credible as a completed-repair record for the insurer.

Do this at intake for every vehicle, not just the ones you expect to become marketing material. You won't know which repair turns into your best Google Business Profile post until it's done, and you can't go back and shoot the "before" once the car's already in primer.

## Why Lighting and Background Consistency Matters More Than Camera Quality

An adjuster or a skeptical customer doesn't need a professional camera. They need a photo that doesn't look edited, staged, or cherry-picked. Shoot in the same spot in your lot or bay for every intake — ideally an area with even overhead light and a plain wall or fence behind the vehicle, not a cluttered shop floor with other cars and tools in frame. Avoid direct sun that throws hard shadows across body panels, since a shadow can visually read as a dent or a scratch that isn't actually there, and that ambiguity is exactly what slows down claim review.

Consistency between the before and after shot matters as much as quality in either one. If the before photo was shot in overcast light and the after photo was shot in bright sun, the color and contrast shift makes the comparison look manipulated even when it isn't — and a customer or adjuster who notices that inconsistency starts questioning everything else in the file. Use the same spot, the same time of day if you can manage it, and the same distance from the vehicle for both shots.

## Handling the File Volume

A single repair order with a full shot list, front and back, plus a few extra angles for a complicated repair, easily runs 20 to 40 photos. At 4-8MB per shot straight off a phone camera, that's 100-300MB per vehicle. Multiply that by however many cars are moving through your shop in a week and you've got a real storage and speed problem, not just a hypothetical one.

That volume hits three places where it actually costs you time:

- **Shop management software** — most platforms (Mitchell, CCC, Body Shop Booster, whatever your shop runs) slow down noticeably once a repair order's photo folder gets large, and some have hard upload size caps per file or per order.
- **Insurance claim portals** — several major carriers cap individual photo uploads at 5-10MB and will silently reject or truncate anything larger, which means someone on your team is re-uploading rejected files instead of writing estimates.
- **Client-facing gallery links** — if you're sending a customer a link to view their vehicle's repair progress, a gallery loaded with full-size phone photos takes noticeably longer to open on their phone, and a slow-loading gallery reads as a slow, disorganized shop even when the actual repair work is on schedule.

The fix isn't shooting fewer photos or lower-resolution ones — you need the full shot list at full detail for the claim file. The fix is compressing the batch before it goes anywhere. ## Getting the Shot List Ready to Use

1. **Shoot the full angle list at intake**, before any repair work starts, in the same lot or bay spot every time.
2. **Crop out anything irrelevant** — other vehicles, tools, staff not involved in the repair — using [Optimage's crop tool](/crop) so the damage and the vehicle stay the clear subject.
3. **Compress the full batch** before it goes into shop management software, a claim portal, or a client gallery link.
4. **File the intake set separately from the marketing set**, even though they're often the same photos — your claim and liability record should stay untouched, while a curated pair goes to Google Business Profile or your website.
5. **Repeat the same angles post-repair** and pair them with the originals for both the claim file and the before/after post.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What angles should I photograph for an insurance claim before starting collision repair?

Shoot all four corners of the vehicle, a wide shot of the full damage area, a close-up of the damage itself, the VIN plate, and the odometer reading. Adjusters use the wide shots to confirm the vehicle and the extent of damage, and the close-up to verify the specific repair being billed.

### Why should a body shop photograph a car before repair even if there's no visible new damage?

Photographing pre-existing dents, scratches, and wear at intake creates a record of the vehicle's condition before it entered the shop. Without that record, a customer can plausibly claim the shop caused damage that was already there, and the shop has no way to disprove it.

### Do I need to compress photos before uploading them to shop management software or a claim portal?

Yes if you're uploading in volume. A typical repair order can carry 20-40 photos, and modern phone cameras produce 4-8MB files by default. Bulk compressing that batch before upload keeps portals and galleries responsive without any visible loss in damage detail.
