**A globally covered celebrity wedding weekend is underway in New York, with dinner events at Madison Square Garden and a level of press attention most photographers will never work under.** This post isn't about the couple — that ground has already been covered, including on this site. It's about what's happening behind the scenes on the photography side, because the workflow a wedding this size requires is a genuinely useful model for any photographer, regardless of who's getting married.

High-profile weddings force a level of discipline around image handling that most photographers never have to think through from scratch, because the cost of getting it wrong — a leaked photo, an early post, a full-resolution file in the wrong hands — is public and immediate. That discipline is worth borrowing even when the stakes are a normal Saturday wedding instead of a stadium's worth of press.

## The NDA Gets Signed Before the Camera Comes Out

Every photographer, second shooter, and assistant working a wedding at this level signs a non-disclosure agreement before the event, not after. It typically covers what can be discussed, what can be shot, where phones and personal cameras are and aren't allowed, and — critically — what happens to the images afterward. Nothing about the day gets posted, described, or delivered outside the agreed process, full stop.

Most wedding photographers will never need language this strict. But the underlying idea — put the image-handling agreement in writing before the event, not as an afterthought in a contract nobody reread — applies to every booking. A one-paragraph clause on delivery timeline, usage rights, and where the final images will live protects both sides and heads off the awkward "can you just email me everything" conversation later.

## Embargo Windows: Nothing Goes Out Until the Couple Says So

The defining feature of a high-profile wedding shoot is the embargo — an agreed window, sometimes running days or weeks past the event, during which nothing gets published or delivered publicly. The couple controls the reveal. Their own announcement, their own timing, their own platform. The photographer's job is to have everything ready the moment that window closes, not to be the one who breaks it.

That's a sharper version of something every wedding photographer already navigates: clients who don't want photos posted to a portfolio or social media before they've shared their own favorites first. Building an explicit embargo date into the delivery timeline — even a modest one, a week or two after the wedding — respects that instinct and removes the ambiguity around when a photographer is free to use the work for their own marketing.

## Proof, Approve, Deliver — the Same Three Steps at Any Scale

Strip away the press and the security detail, and a high-profile wedding's image pipeline is the same three-step structure any photographer can run.

1. **Watermarked, lower-resolution proofs go to the client first.** Nobody outside the couple's inner circle sees a full-resolution, unwatermarked image before it's been reviewed and approved. This protects the work in progress and gives the client a low-pressure way to pick favorites without worrying about an unfinished edit leaking.
2. **The client approves a selection, not the whole shoot.** A wedding produces thousands of frames; the client doesn't need or want all of them at full resolution. Approval narrows the delivery to the images that are actually going to be used, printed, or shared.
3. **Full-resolution files go out through a private gallery link, not an attachment.** A secure, password- or link-gated gallery replaces the emailed zip file entirely — no file-size limits to work around, no risk of the wrong recipient forwarding a raw attachment, and a delivery experience that feels considered rather than dumped.

## Why a Non-Celebrity Client Wants This Too

Nobody booking a normal wedding photographer is worried about tabloids. But every client wants their photos to feel handled — reviewed with intention, delivered somewhere that feels like theirs, not buried in a Google Drive folder shared with a dozen other people's weddings by accident. A proof-approve-deliver structure gives a $3,000 wedding package the same shape as a wedding with a security detail, minus the NDA and the embargo. It's a better client experience and it looks more professional in the process — a private gallery with the couple's names on it reads as a finished product, where a zip file reads as a data dump.

It also protects the photographer. Full-resolution files sitting in an inbox or a shared folder are trivially easy to redistribute before final payment clears or before the photographer has had a chance to select their own portfolio pieces. A gated gallery link keeps that control in the photographer's hands until the delivery is actually meant to happen.
