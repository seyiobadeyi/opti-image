**AEW's All In: London returns to Wembley Stadium today, August 30, with roughly 45,000 fans in attendance — the third-biggest crowd in AEW's seven-year history, behind only the 2023 and 2024 Wembley editions of the same event.** A stadium wrestling show is one of the toughest live events to photograph well, for a reason most fans don't think about until they're actually there: the ring is a tiny fixed point in an enormous bowl, and the show around it cycles through wildly different lighting multiple times per match.

## Why Stadium Wrestling Beats Most Live Events for Photo Difficulty

At an arena show, the ring occupies a meaningful chunk of the room and most seats have a real view. At Wembley, the ring is a small, brightly lit square in the middle of a stadium built for football — most of the 45,000 people there are seeing the actual wrestlers as small figures and following the show through the big screens as much as the live action, which is exactly the problem your camera runs into too. A phone camera pointed at the ring from anywhere beyond the lower bowl captures a lit square with tiny figures in it, unless you're deliberately using zoom, and even then digital zoom on a phone degrades fast past a certain point.

The lighting adds a second layer of difficulty on top of the distance problem. A single entrance can run through spotlight darkness, a full pyro burst, then settle into even ring lighting for the actual match — three distinct exposure situations inside maybe 90 seconds, and auto mode struggles to keep up with any of them cleanly.

## Shooting Each Part of the Show

1. **Entrances with pyro:** Lock shutter speed fast, 1/500s minimum, and underexpose slightly going in. Pyro is genuinely bright but brief — a slow shutter either blows the flare into a white blob or smears it, and it's far easier to brighten a slightly dark, sharp pyro shot afterward than to recover one that's already blown out.
2. **Spotlight entrance walks:** These are high-contrast — a bright figure against a dark stadium. Expose for the wrestler, not the background; let the crowd around them go dark, since trying to brighten the whole frame just makes the entrance figure overexposed and washed out.
3. **The actual match under ring lighting:** This is the closest to a normal even-lit scene. Same rules as any live action — fast shutter, don't rely on night mode, burst shoot and pick the best frame.
4. **Wide crowd shots:** This is where you can slow down and use your phone's normal or night mode — a static wide shot of 45,000 people benefits from the extra light-gathering a longer exposure gives you, since nothing in the frame is moving fast enough to blur.

## Where Wembley Fits in AEW's History

The top three crowds in AEW's history are now all Wembley shows — 2023's over 70,000, 2024's edition, and today's roughly 45,000. That the company keeps returning to the same stadium and keeps drawing at that scale says something about how quickly AEW built a European audience willing to travel for a single stadium date, even without matching its own record every year. A show that doesn't set a new attendance record can still be a genuinely massive event by any other promotion's standard — worth keeping in perspective before writing today's number off as a letdown.
