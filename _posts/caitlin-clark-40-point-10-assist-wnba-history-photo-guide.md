**Caitlin Clark scored a career-high 45 points with 10 assists on July 18, becoming the first player in WNBA history to post a 40-point, 10-assist game, in a 110-107 Indiana Fever win over the Seattle Storm.** She did it in 29 minutes, still on a minutes restriction, scoring 16 of Indiana's final 19 points after the Fever trailed by 8 in the fourth quarter. If you were in the building or watching live and pulled up a phone photo afterward, there's a good chance it looked noticeably worse than the highlight clip already circulating — and that's not really about your camera skill.

## The Line That Makes This Historic

Forty points and 10 assists have never happened together in a single WNBA game before Friday night. Clark's previous career high was 35; she broke it by 10 points while also running the offense well enough to rack up double-digit assists, a combination that usually trades off against each other — a big scoring night tends to mean fewer looks created for teammates, not more. She hit 11 of 18 shots, 6 of 10 from three, and set career bests in free throws made and attempted (17 of 19) in the process.

## Why the Broadcast Clip Looks Better Than Your Photo

Every indoor arena runs lighting built for television, not for the thousands of phone cameras pointed at the court from angles the broadcast never planned for. Broadcast cameras are white-balanced live by an engineer sitting at a truck outside the building, calibrated to that specific rig, and they carry sensors several times the size of a phone's — which means far less noise at the ISO levels a fast-moving night game demands. A phone camera in the same building has to guess the white balance automatically, usually errs toward a cooler, flatter read on mixed arena lighting, and pushes its ISO higher to freeze the action, which is exactly what produces the grainy, slightly green-tinted look a lot of fan photos come out with.

None of that means your photo is unusable. It means the raw file needs correction the broadcast feed already got for free.

1. **Shoot in burst mode during any fast sequence**, since Clark's fourth-quarter run happened in seconds and a single frame is a guess at the peak moment.
2. **Correct white balance after the fact rather than trusting the camera's live guess.** Arena lighting rigs mix sodium and LED sources unevenly across the court, and auto white balance frequently splits the difference badly.
3. **Don't crank exposure to fix a dark photo — it just adds more noise on top of what high ISO already introduced.** A modest brightness lift plus targeted noise reduction holds detail better than a blanket exposure boost.
4. **Compress before sharing**, especially for a moment already going viral. A crisp, corrected image at a reasonable file size will actually load in a group chat faster than a large, uncorrected one competing with everyone else's photos of the same night.
