**Taylor Farms has pulled iceberg lettuce sourced from central Mexico out of the U.S. market across 27 states after a cyclospora outbreak tied to more than 1,600 illnesses and 141 hospitalizations, with the affected product having moved through the supply chain between June 29 and July 16.** That's a three-week distribution window, which is long enough that any grocer, restaurant, or food service operator who received the product needs more than "we think we pulled it" on file. They need a photographed, dated record of exactly what came off the shelf and when.

An FDA test on one sample from a recalled lot later came back a false positive for the parasite — a real, reported detail of this specific outbreak investigation. That's not a knock on the FDA's process; false positives happen and get corrected. It is a clear illustration of why a business's own records matter independent of what any single lab result eventually shows. If a health inspector, an insurer, or a customer's attorney asks six weeks from now exactly which lettuce, from which delivery, was pulled on which date, "we're pretty sure it was the batch that came in around then" is a materially weaker answer than a folder of timestamped photos showing the lot code, the shelf tag, and the product going into the trash.

## What Actually Belongs in the Photo Record

1. **The shelf tag or case label showing the lot number**, photographed before the product is touched or moved. Lot codes are usually small print — a fast phone photo close enough to read the code clearly beats a wide shot that requires zooming in later and losing resolution.
2. **The product itself in its original packaging**, showing the brand and any date codes, in case the recall notice's specifics get updated after the fact (which happened here, with the FDA's false-positive correction).
3. **The disposal or return process**, whether that's product going into a marked bin, being boxed for return to a distributor, or logged in a POS system — a photo of the physical handoff closes the loop on "we pulled it" with proof it actually left the building.
4. **A timestamp that's actually reliable.** A phone photo's built-in timestamp metadata is more defensible than a handwritten note added after the fact, provided the file isn't later stripped of that metadata before it needs to be produced.

## The Metadata Tension Worth Knowing About

This is the one place where Optimage's usual advice — strip EXIF metadata before sharing a photo — runs the other direction. For a public-facing photo, embedded timestamp and location data is often a privacy risk worth removing. For an internal compliance record, that same timestamp is the thing that makes the photo useful as evidence. Keep recall-documentation photos in a private, unedited folder with metadata intact, separate from anything getting compressed and posted publicly. If a photo from that same shoot ever needs to go out in a public statement or social post, that's the moment to run it through a compressor and strip identifying metadata — not before.

[Optimage's compressor](/compress) is there for the public-facing side of this once you need it; the internal compliance copies should stay untouched.

## What to Do Before the Next Recall Notice Lands

Build the habit now, not during the next active recall: designate one phone or one shared folder for recall documentation, photograph lot codes and disposal every time, and keep those files with metadata intact and separate from anything meant for public sharing. The Taylor Farms recall will resolve one way or another. The next one is only a matter of time, and the businesses that handle it cleanly are the ones that already had this habit before they needed it.
