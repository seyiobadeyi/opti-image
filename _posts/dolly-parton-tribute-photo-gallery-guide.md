Most of that will be gone from easy view within a week, buried under new posts. A little intention now turns it into something that actually lasts.

## Why a Social Feed Isn't a Real Archive

A tribute post gets its moment — likes, shares, comments — and then it's exactly as findable as anything else in a feed that never stops moving: not very. Worse, every repost degrades the image a little further. A concert photo shot on film decades ago and scanned once, shared and reshared across platforms for a week, comes out the other side visibly worse than where it started — compression artifacts stacking on compression artifacts, each platform re-processing an already-processed file. If a photo matters enough to want later, "posted it once" isn't the same as "kept it."

## What a Tribute Collection Usually Looks Like

Anyone who's been a fan for decades has a genuinely mixed photo set: a phone photo from a show three years ago, a scanned print from a concert in the 1990s, a screenshot of a favorite post, maybe a photo of a signed album cover. That mix is the whole point — it's the range of a long relationship with an artist, not a single format or era. It's also exactly the kind of inconsistent file set that looks messy and disjointed if you just dump it into a folder or a group chat as-is. A little standardizing turns "a pile of mismatched files" into something that reads as an actual, coherent collection.

## Putting a Real Archive Together

1. **Ask everyone contributing for their original file, not a re-share.** A photo pulled directly from someone's camera roll or an old scan folder will always be higher quality than the version that's already bounced through three platforms.
2. **Don't worry about matching formats or sizes up front.** Old scans, phone photos, and screenshots can all coexist in one collection — [Optimage's convert tool](/convert) can normalize the whole batch to one consistent format afterward.
3. **Compress for sharing, not for the archive copy.** Keep an untouched original of anything that matters, and generate a compressed version specifically for the gallery or for sending around — [Optimage's compress tool](/compress) does that without visibly degrading the image.
4. **Put it all in one shared, organized place.** [A free shared gallery](/galleries) lets a family, a fan group, or a community collect and view a tribute collection together, rather than everyone holding a few scattered photos on their own phone.
5. **Label what you can.** A show date, a city, a year — even rough details make a collection meaningfully more useful later than an unlabeled folder of "old photos."

## This Isn't Just About One Loss

The instinct to dig up old photos and share them happens every time someone significant is lost — a family member, a friend, a public figure whose work meant something personal. The tools and the process are the same regardless of scale: gather the originals while people still have them close at hand, standardize the mixed formats without destroying quality, and put the collection somewhere it'll still be easy to find in five years, not just easy to find in the group chat this week.

## Start a Collection Before the Photos Scatter

If you or your community are already sharing old photos this week, don't let that momentum dissipate into a dozen separate camera rolls. [Set up a free shared gallery](/galleries) now, while everyone's already looking for photos to contribute, and it becomes the one place the whole collection actually lives.
