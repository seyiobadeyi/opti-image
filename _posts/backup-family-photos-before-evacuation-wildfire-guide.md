**Wildfires forced more than 360,000 people to evacuate across France and Spain in late July 2026.** Whenever evacuations happen at that scale, the same pattern shows up afterward: people talk about the pets they carried out, the documents they grabbed, and, almost universally, the photos they wish they'd saved. This isn't a piece about that disaster. It's a practical guide to making sure photos are never the thing you're scrambling for when an evacuation order comes, whether the threat is wildfire, hurricane, or flood.

## Why Photos Are the Thing People Regret Losing Most

Ask anyone who's been through a house fire, a flood, or a wildfire evacuation what they wish they'd grabbed, and photos come up more than almost anything else. Not the TV, not the furniture, not even most of the electronics. Insurance replaces a couch. Nothing replaces the only photo you have of a grandparent who's no longer alive, or the box of prints from a childhood before anyone in the family owned a digital camera.

That's precisely why photos deserve a backup plan that doesn't depend on you remembering to grab a box on your way out the door. If protecting them requires a clear head and thirty spare minutes during an active evacuation, the plan has already failed — evacuations don't offer either.

## The Problem With Relying on Just Your Phone, or Just Your Prints

Most people's photo backup strategy is really no strategy at all — it's "the photos are on my phone" or "the photos are in an album in the closet." Both feel safe until the exact moment they aren't.

A phone gets left charging in another room during a middle-of-the-night evacuation, or dropped, or lost in the chaos of loading a car in the dark. Even when it makes it out safely, a phone synced to a cloud account you can't log into, or damaged before you back it up, isn't really a backup — it's a single copy that happens to also be your phone.

Physical prints and albums have the opposite failure mode: they don't move at all. If the house burns or floods, whatever was inside it goes with it, and a shoebox of irreplaceable prints is exactly as vulnerable as the walls around it. Scanning helps, but only if the digital copy lives somewhere other than the same house, tied to the same single point of failure.

The fix isn't picking phone over prints or prints over phone. It's making sure there's a copy that exists independently of both — stored somewhere that survives even if the phone and the house don't.

## Build the Habit Before Fire Season Peaks, Not During an Evacuation

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that matters most: do this now, while there's no smoke on the horizon and no order to leave. Fire and hurricane seasons have predictable peaks — pick a date before that peak hits and treat backing up your photo library as a routine task, like changing a smoke detector battery.

During an active evacuation you'll have none of what this actually requires: no time, often no cell signal, and no patience for a multi-gigabyte upload over a struggling connection while you're also loading a car and checking on family. A routine that only works under ideal conditions is a routine that fails exactly when it counts.

## Do This Before You Have to Leave

This is the core routine. It takes one afternoon, and once it's done, updating it later only takes minutes.

1. **Pick one place all your photos will live outside your phone and your house.** A private cloud gallery works better than a general-purpose cloud drive here, because it's built specifically for photos and easy to share as one link.
2. **Gather everything into a single folder first** — your phone's camera roll, any photos on an old laptop or hard drive, and scans of physical prints you've already digitized. Don't try to sort or curate yet; just get it all in one place.
3. **Compress the whole batch before you upload anything.** A full phone library at original resolution is often tens of gigabytes, and every gigabyte you don't need to upload is time you save later. [Optimage compresses photos in bulk for free](/compress), directly in your browser, with no account required and no limit that forces you to split a large library into small batches.
4. **Upload the compressed library to your private gallery.** Because the files are smaller, this finishes in minutes rather than hours, even on an ordinary home connection.
5. **Create one shareable link to the gallery** and send it to the people in your household or extended family who'd need access if your phone were unavailable.
6. **Save that link somewhere that isn't only on your phone** — in an email you can pull up from any device, written down with your other emergency documents, or shared directly with a family member who lives elsewhere.
7. **Set a recurring reminder to add new photos** before each fire or storm season peak, so the backup doesn't quietly go stale for a year or two between updates.

## Why Compression Isn't Optional Here

Skipping the compression step is the single most common reason people abandon a backup halfway through. A phone camera shoots HEIC or JPEG at full sensor resolution by default, and a library built up over several years routinely lands in the tens of gigabytes. Upload that over a typical home connection — usually slower for uploads than downloads — and you're looking at hours, sometimes overnight, with a real chance it stalls or you give up partway through.

Compress that same library first and most of it shrinks by half or more with no visible quality loss, since the resolution a phone captures at is far higher than anything you'd notice on a phone screen or in a shared gallery. That turns an overnight ordeal into one evening with no urgency attached — exactly the condition under which a backup routine actually gets finished instead of postponed again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the best way to back up family photos before an evacuation?**
Compress your full photo library and upload it to a private cloud gallery weeks before fire or storm season peaks, then share one link with family members so everyone can access the photos from any phone. Doing this in advance matters because there's rarely time, signal, or bandwidth to do it during an actual evacuation.

**Is it enough to just have photos backed up on my phone?**
No. A phone can be lost, damaged, left behind in a rushed evacuation, or run out of battery with no way to charge it. Phone-only backup and prints-only backup share the same flaw — both are a single copy in a single location that can be destroyed or separated from you at the exact moment you need it.

**Why does compressing photos before uploading matter for this routine?**
A typical modern phone library runs into tens of gigabytes at full camera resolution, and uploading that much data over a home connection can take hours. Compressing the library first cuts most of that size with no visible quality loss, turning an overnight upload into something you can finish in one sitting, well before you'd ever need to rely on it.
