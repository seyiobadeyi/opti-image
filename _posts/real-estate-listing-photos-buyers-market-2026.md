**Existing-home inventory has climbed to around 4.5 months of supply in 2026 — enough that buyers, for the first time in years, actually have listings to compare instead of grabbing whatever's available.** The median sales price still set a record at $440,600 in June, so this isn't a market that's collapsed for sellers. But it is a market where a buyer opens a search, gets ten reasonably similar homes in their price range and area, and has to decide fast which ones are worth a second look. Photos are doing that filtering job now more than they have in years.

## Why Tight Inventory Used to Cover for Bad Photos

When there's nothing else on the market, a buyer tolerates a mediocre listing gallery because the alternative is not seeing the house at all. That's not true anymore in most markets. With months of supply back up near historically normal levels, a buyer scrolling a search results page is genuinely choosing between comparable options, and the ones with dark, cluttered, or slow-loading photos lose that comparison before the buyer even reads the square footage.

This isn't a subtle shift. It's the difference between photos as a formality and photos as the actual first round of buyer screening.

## Where Listings Actually Lose Buyers on Photos

Most agents aren't taking bad photos — the framing and lighting on professionally shot listings is usually fine. The problem is almost always what happens after the shoot, in the upload step nobody thinks about:

1. **Camera-original files that are too big for how buyers actually browse.** A DSLR or a modern phone shoots 20-40MP photos that run 6-10MB each. Uploaded straight to an MLS or a listing site, a gallery of 25 of those photos loads noticeably slower on mobile than a compressed equivalent — and mobile is where most home searches start now.
2. **Inconsistent aspect ratios across the gallery.** A mix of wide-angle interior shots, portrait exterior shots, and cropped detail photos reads as unpolished when a buyer flips through quickly, even if each individual photo is well composed.
3. **PNG files mixed into a photo gallery.** Some listing photos end up saved as PNG from staging or editing software, which produces unnecessarily large files for photographic content compared to JPEG at equivalent visual quality.
4. **No compression pass before the batch upload.** Doing this one photo at a time across a full listing gallery is the reason it often doesn't get done — bulk compression across the whole folder takes roughly the same effort as compressing five images, not twenty-five times the effort.

## Comparison Table: Original vs. Optimized Listing Gallery

| | Camera-original upload | Optimized upload |
|---|---|---|
| Average file size (25 photos) | 6-10MB each, 150-250MB total | 200-400KB each, 5-10MB total |
| Mobile gallery load time | Several seconds per photo | Near-instant |
| Aspect ratio consistency | Whatever the camera shot | Standardized across the gallery |
| Buyer drop-off risk | Higher, especially on slow connections | Lower |

## The Market Rewarded Sloppy Photos Before. It Doesn't Now.

None of this requires a bigger photography budget or a different photographer. It requires treating the upload step as part of the listing process instead of an afterthought — compressing the batch, standardizing the aspect ratios, and making sure nothing goes up as an oversized PNG. In a market where buyers have nine other listings open in other tabs, that's the difference between a gallery that gets fully viewed and one that gets abandoned three photos in.
