**Home values have gone flat or dropped outright in 22 of the 50 largest U.S. metro areas this year, with Austin down 5.7%, Las Vegas down 3.2%, and Tampa down 2.8% — a real shift after years where sellers set the terms.** Nationally the typical home is worth $372,057, up a modest 1.1% from a year ago, and mortgage rates sitting around 6.5% are actually a touch below where they were last summer. None of that sounds dramatic on its own, but put together it means something concrete for anyone buying or selling right now: the inspection report has real leverage behind it again, and how well that report is photographed determines how much of that leverage you actually get to use.

## Why the Inspection Suddenly Matters More

For several years, tight inventory and bidding wars meant a lot of buyers waived inspection contingencies entirely just to get an offer accepted, or treated the inspection as a formality rather than a negotiating tool. That's changing. With more inventory sitting on the market in a growing number of metros and price growth barely keeping pace with inflation nationally, buyers have room to actually use what the inspection turns up — asking for repairs, a price reduction, or a credit at closing, instead of just accepting the property as-is to win the deal.

That only works if the inspection findings are documented well enough to survive a conversation with the seller's agent. A general contractor or listing agent pushing back on a repair request will look for any excuse to characterize a defect as minor or already-known. A wide, well-lit photo with a tight, sharp close-up next to it doesn't leave much room for that argument. A single dark, blurry phone photo does.

## The Two-Shot Rule Every Good Inspector Already Follows

Professional inspectors who've been doing this for years tend to converge on the same basic pattern, whether or not their report software enforces it:

1. **A wide establishing shot.** This shows where the defect is — which wall, which corner of the crawlspace, which section of roofline — so there's no ambiguity about location when the report gets reviewed weeks later, possibly by someone who never walked the property.
2. **A tight close-up of the actual damage.** Cracking, staining, corrosion, wear — whatever it is, this shot needs to be sharp enough that a contractor could bid the repair from the photo alone, without a second site visit.

Skipping either half of that pair weakens the finding. A close-up with no context photo leaves the seller's side able to argue it's out of scale or somewhere irrelevant. A wide shot with no close-up doesn't actually prove the severity of what's being flagged.

## Where Most Reports Fall Apart

The photography usually isn't the weak point — most inspectors and plenty of buyers walking through with them know how to point a camera at a problem. The weak point is what happens after: dozens of photos exported into a PDF report at whatever compression the report software defaults to, filenames like IMG_4471.jpg with no indication of what they show, and a final document that's either so heavily compressed the defects are hard to make out on a screen, or so bloated with unoptimized originals that it's a pain to email or upload to a lender portal.

- **Compress deliberately, not by default.** Report software often auto-compresses embedded images aggressively to keep file size down, sometimes past the point where a hairline crack or moisture stain is still visible. Export or attach separate full-resolution copies of anything that matters for negotiation.
- **Rename before you file, not after.** "Crack — foundation, NE corner" beats IMG_4471.jpg every time someone has to find the photo again in a dispute six weeks later.
- **Keep originals, not phone-screen screenshots.** A screenshot of a photo already inside a PDF has been recompressed twice and lost whatever metadata timestamp proved when it was taken. Pull the source file if you need to reference it seriously.

If you're buying or selling in a market where values are actually moving — down in Austin or Vegas, flat almost everywhere else — the inspection is doing more negotiating work than it has in years, and it's worth treating the photo documentation with the same seriousness as the report's written findings.
