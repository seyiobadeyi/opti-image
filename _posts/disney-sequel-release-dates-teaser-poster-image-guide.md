**Disney set theatrical release dates for both Lilo & Stitch 2 and The Incredibles 3 this week, and neither announcement came with a single new photo, poster, or frame of footage.** That's a normal part of how studios schedule tentpole sequels years in advance — a release date locks in a marketing calendar long before any actual production imagery exists to promote it. It also creates a small, recurring problem for everyone whose job is to cover that announcement visually: there's a real news story with genuine audience interest, and nothing new to show alongside it.

## The Announcement-Without-Assets Problem

A release-date announcement is newsworthy on its own — fans want to know when a sequel to a beloved film is actually coming out — but it's not accompanied by the usual press kit of stills and key art that a trailer drop or full marketing push would include. That leaves anyone covering the story, from major entertainment outlets to fan accounts, with one option: illustrate the piece using imagery from the original film. It's not misleading as long as it's clearly the earlier movie's art, but it does mean a huge number of articles covering the exact same announcement end up reaching for the exact same handful of years-old promotional photos.

## Why the Old Stills Often Look Wrong in a New Layout

Promotional images from a film's original release cycle were sized for that era's standard web and print use — often smaller than what a modern article's hero image slot, social share card, or video thumbnail actually needs. When a site or fan account drops one of those older stills into a header space designed for a larger, higher-resolution image, the software stretches it to fit, and stretching introduces visible softness and blocky artifacts that weren't there in the source file at its native size. It's a small technical detail, but it's the difference between an announcement post that looks clean and one that looks visibly low-effort next to competitors covering the same story.

## Getting the Most Out of an Old Promotional Still

1. **Check the source resolution before placing it in a large layout slot.** An image that looked fine as a small thumbnail years ago may not have the pixel dimensions to fill a full-width modern hero image without stretching.
2. **Crop tighter instead of scaling up.** A tighter crop on a smaller original image keeps every pixel sharp; stretching the same image to fill a wider space just makes the softness more visible, not less.
3. **Match the file format to where it's going.** A still headed for a fast-loading article header benefits from a modern compressed format rather than whatever legacy format it was originally distributed in.
4. **Compress consistently across every image in the piece.** An article mixing a crisp new social graphic with a soft, over-stretched old still reads as inconsistent even if each individual choice made sense in isolation.

## The Pattern Repeats Every Time a Sequel Gets Dated Early

This isn't unique to these two films — it's the standard shape of early sequel announcements across every studio. A date gets set for scheduling and marketing-calendar reasons long before there's anything new to actually show, and the visual coverage of that announcement runs entirely on recycled material until a teaser eventually arrives. Anyone covering entertainment news regularly is going to hit this exact situation again before the year's out, and the same fix — check the source resolution, crop instead of stretch, compress consistently — applies every time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Did Disney release any new images for Lilo & Stitch 2 or The Incredibles 3?**
No. Both films had theatrical release dates announced without accompanying stills, key art, teaser posters, or footage, meaning any coverage of the news has to be illustrated using existing promotional material from the earlier films rather than new assets.

**Why do old promotional stills often look soft when reused in new articles?**
Promotional images from years-old film campaigns were often distributed at resolutions sized for the web standards of that release period, which are frequently smaller than what current article headers, social cards, or thumbnail specs require, causing visible softness when the image is stretched up to fit a larger space.
