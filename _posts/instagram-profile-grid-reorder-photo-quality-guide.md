**Instagram's grid finally works the way people have been asking for since 2013: drag any post, from any point in your account's history, into any position.** The feature started rolling out June 8 and has been expanding since. Before this, your only control over grid order was the 3-pin feature — lock up to three posts at the top, and everything else fell back to strict chronological order underneath them.

That was always a strange limitation for anyone using Instagram as a portfolio, a storefront, or a brand presence rather than a diary. A photographer's best shot from three years ago was permanently buried unless it happened to also be recent. Now it isn't. You can build the grid you actually want, top to bottom, regardless of when anything was posted.

## The Catch Nobody's Talking About

Here's the thing reorder exposes that chronological order used to hide: **a photo's visibility used to be capped by its age. Now it's only capped by where you decide to put it.**

That sounds like pure upside, and mostly it is. But it means every photo you drag into a top-row position gets scrutinized at a level it was never designed for. A photo from 2021, uploaded before you had a real workflow, compressed by whatever Instagram's pipeline looked like at the time, sitting quietly at post #340 where nobody scrolls — that same photo dragged into position #2 is now the second thing anyone sees on your profile, viewed at whatever resolution the visitor's screen renders it at, right next to your sharpest recent work.

Chronological order was, in a strange way, forgiving. Weak photos got a built-in excuse — "it's old." Now that excuse is gone, because you personally chose where it sits.

## What to Do Before You Rebuild Your Grid

Don't just start dragging. Do this first:

1. **Scroll your full grid and shortlist your actual best 9-12 photos** — the number that fills a typical mobile screen on a profile visit. This is your new top block regardless of when they were posted.
2. **Zoom into each shortlisted photo on your phone at full screen.** Look specifically for compression blockiness in shadows, banding in skies or gradients, and softness around edges — the three tells of an old, over-compressed upload.
3. **For anything that fails that check, don't drag the original into a top position.** Re-export a clean version from your original file (or the best copy you still have) at WebP or JPEG quality 85-90, post it fresh, and use reorder to slot it where the old one would have gone. Yes, it starts at zero likes. A visibly compressed photo in your second grid tile costs you more than that.
4. **For anything you can't re-source** (you no longer have the original file), either leave it lower in the grid where scrutiny is lower, or accept the quality hit if the photo itself is worth it — a blurry candid of a moment that matters is still better in position #4 than a sharp photo of nothing.
5. **Going forward, export everything at consistent settings** so this problem doesn't recur. WebP, minimum 1080px, quality 85-90 is the right baseline for anything destined for a portfolio grid.

## Why This Matters More for Some Accounts Than Others

If your Instagram is personal and casual, none of this matters — reorder it however feels right and move on. But if your grid functions as a portfolio (photographers, designers, small brands) or a storefront (product sellers, service businesses), the top block of your grid is effectively your homepage now. It's the first thing a potential client or customer sees before they decide whether to keep scrolling or tap follow. Chronological order used to make that decision partly for you. Now it's entirely a choice, which means the quality bar for what you choose is entirely your responsibility too.

The mechanical fix is simple: run your shortlisted photos through an [image compressor](/compress) set to WebP at quality 85-90 before you upload, so you're handing Instagram's own compression pipeline the best possible input rather than a file that's already lost detail once. It costs five minutes per photo and it's the difference between a top-row image that holds up when someone taps to view it full-screen and one that visibly falls apart.
