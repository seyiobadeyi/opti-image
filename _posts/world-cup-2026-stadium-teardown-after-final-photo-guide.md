**The confetti's been swept up, the trophy's been lifted, and while most of the internet is still working through its backlog of final photos, stadium crews across the US, Mexico, and Canada are already dismantling a tournament's worth of temporary infrastructure.** It's the least photographed part of the entire World Cup, which is exactly why it's worth a look before it's gone — this phase of a host venue's life lasts days, not weeks, and almost nobody outside official channels documents it.

## What Actually Comes Down First

Several of this tournament's US host venues were NFL stadiums, retrofitted with temporary natural-grass surfaces laid directly over the standard turf to meet FIFA's playing-surface requirements. That grass has to come out first, often within days of a venue's last match, so the building can go back to hosting football season on schedule. Alongside it: FIFA branding wrapped around every visible surface, broadcast camera platforms and cabling runs that took weeks to install, temporary premium seating added for the tournament, and sponsor overlays on everything from concourse walls to goal-line padding. None of this is built to stay. Most of it is designed from day one to come apart fast, because the venue's actual owner needs the building back for its regular tenant.

Every match generated a flood of fan photos — that's the entire premise behind this tournament setting a photo-and-video sharing record across three countries and 104 games. Almost none of that same crowd of photographers is inside these venues during teardown, because there's no ticketed event to attend. That leaves the visual record of this phase almost entirely to press access and official archival photography, which is a much thinner, more centralized record than the crowdsourced abundance every match produced. If you were credentialed staff, a vendor, or anyone with legitimate access during this window, the photos you take right now are documenting something the internet's usual flood of content will otherwise barely capture.

## What's Actually Worth Photographing in This Window

1. **The temporary grass coming up**, if you have access — a detail almost nobody outside groundskeeping crews ever sees, and one that visually explains exactly how an NFL stadium became a World Cup pitch in the first place.
2. **Branding removal in progress**, half-stripped signage and exposed original stadium surfaces underneath — a genuinely unusual in-between state that only exists for a few days.
3. **Empty seating bowls with sponsor overlays still up but no crowd** — a stark, useful contrast image against every packed-stadium photo from the tournament itself.
4. **Before-the-Cup versus during-the-Cup versus after-the-Cup comparisons**, if you or your organization photographed the same venue at each stage — a genuinely rare set to have and a strong retrospective asset going forward.

## What to Take From This

The version of these stadiums that existed for the last month is already disappearing, faster than most of the internet's attention span is moving on from the final itself. If you have any access to it, this window — not the next World Cup buildup four years out — is the actual chance to document what a tournament venue looks like coming apart.
