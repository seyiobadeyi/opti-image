**The 2026 World Cup opened on June 11 at a revamped Azteca Stadium in Mexico City, built around papel picado — Mexico's centuries-old tradition of intricately cut tissue paper — rather than the generic fireworks-and-lasers formula most recent opening ceremonies have defaulted to.** More than 80,000 fans watched Shakira and Burna Boy debut the official tournament song "Dai Dai," alongside a lineup that included Alejandro Fernandez, Belinda, Danny Ocean, J Balvin, Lila Downs, Los Angeles Azules, Mana, and Tyla, ahead of Mexico's opening match against South Africa. Two and a half months out, it's worth looking at how that specific creative choice actually held up in photographs, because a craft-based visual theme is a genuinely different photography problem than the ceremonies that came before it.

## Why Papel Picado Was a Bolder Choice Than It Sounds

Recent World Cup opening ceremonies have leaned heavily on scale as the point — massive LED displays, synchronized drone shows, pyrotechnics timed to a pop anthem. That formula photographs reliably: bright, high-contrast, easy to capture well even from a mediocre seat or a phone camera, because the light sources are doing most of the work. Papel picado is the opposite kind of visual. It's built on fine detail, intricate cut patterns, and color that depends on how it's lit rather than generating its own brightness. Pulling that off at stadium scale — enough papel picado-inspired design to fill a bowl holding 80,000-plus people and read clearly on broadcast — was a real production risk that a straightforward lights-and-fireworks show doesn't carry.

It paid off specifically because Mexico leaned into the theme's actual texture rather than abstracting it into something generic. Photos from the ceremony show pattern and layering that a fireworks show simply doesn't produce — the kind of image that holds up as a poster or a print in a way a photo of a pyrotechnic burst usually doesn't, because the interest is in the design itself rather than a fleeting moment of light.

## The Photography Tradeoff Nobody Talks About

Every opening ceremony faces the same basic split: what looks best on a broadcast camera with professional lighting control is rarely identical to what looks best from a phone in the stands. A pyrotechnics-heavy show is forgiving of that gap — bright bursts against a dark stadium sky read fine on both a broadcast feed and a phone photo, because the light source is doing the work regardless of the capturing device. A texture-and-pattern-based show like this one is much less forgiving. Fine papel picado detail that a broadcast camera with the right lens and lighting captures beautifully can turn into a blurry, underexposed mess on a phone photo from an upper-deck seat, especially once motion and crowd noise are added to the mix.

That gap matters more now than it did a decade ago, because the ceremony's actual afterlife online is overwhelmingly made up of fan-shot phone photos and video, not broadcast stills. A ceremony designed around a visual theme that photographs beautifully on professional equipment but poorly on a phone risks having its actual public memory shaped by the worse version of the images, not the better one.

## What This Means for Anyone Archiving Ceremony Photos

If you were there, or you're pulling together a personal or fan archive of tournament photos that includes ceremony shots, the texture-heavy nature of this particular ceremony means the photos are more sensitive to compression than a typical bright, high-contrast stadium shot would be:

1. **Avoid over-compressing detail-heavy ceremony photos.** Aggressive compression on fine papel picado patterning shows up as visible blocking faster than it would on a simpler image — [compress at a setting that preserves detail](/compress) rather than defaulting to maximum size reduction.
2. **Keep a higher-resolution copy of any ceremony shot you actually like.** The detail that makes these photos worth keeping is exactly what gets lost first if you compress too aggressively or downsize too far.
3. **Sort ceremony photos separately from match photos** when building an archive — they're a genuinely different visual category and get lost easily when mixed into a folder of hundreds of in-game shots.
