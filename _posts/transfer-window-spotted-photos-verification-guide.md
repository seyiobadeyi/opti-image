**Liverpool are reportedly preparing to move for Bradley Barcola after receiving "encouragement" from the PSG winger's camp — and within hours, the usual wave of grainy sighting photos started circulating before either club had said a word.** That's the actual shape of a modern transfer rumor: not a report, but an image. A blurry figure at an airport, a car with tinted windows outside a clinic, a hotel lobby shot from across the room. None of it confirms anything, and almost none of it holds up to five minutes of checking.

## The Rumor Photo Is a Different Species From the Announcement Photo

There are two completely different image economies running through every transfer window, and they rarely overlap. The rumor economy runs on speed and ambiguity — a photo doesn't need to be clear, it needs to exist, get retweeted, and generate a caption arguing about what it shows. The confirmation economy runs on the opposite instinct: once a move is actually done, the club's media team takes over entirely, and every trace of the leaked, blurry version vanishes, replaced by a lit, cropped, on-brand sequence shot on a body that costs more than most fans' cars.

## How to Actually Check a "Spotted" Photo

Most fake or recycled transfer photos fall apart under the same three checks, in this order:

1. **Reverse image search the photo itself.** Google Lens or TinEye will surface earlier posts using the same image. A striking number of "exclusive" sighting photos are two- or three-year-old images of a completely different player at the same airport, recirculated because tinted-window sedans and jet-bridge photos are generic enough to pass at a glance.
2. **Check the metadata, if any survives.** Screenshots and re-uploads strip EXIF data almost immediately, but an original photo still carrying a timestamp will either match or contradict the claimed date instantly. No metadata at all isn't proof of fakery, but it removes the easiest way to confirm the photo.
3. **Look at what's actually in frame.** Real sighting photos tend to be badly composed — shot through a fence, over someone's shoulder, at an angle that makes sense for someone hiding a phone. A suspiciously well-framed, well-lit "candid" is usually staged or sourced from an official event gallery, not an actual leak.

## Why the Announcement Photo Looks So Different

Once a deal is done, none of the leaked material survives contact with the club's own release. The three-shot format we've covered before — shirt hold, stadium walkout, tunnel close-up — gets shot on a 45-megapixel body specifically so it can be cropped into every platform's native aspect ratio without quality loss. Every rumor photo that drove three days of speculation gets buried under one official image within minutes of the announcement post going live.

## If You're Sharing Transfer Photos Yourself

Fans re-sharing "spotted" photos in group chats and forums run into the same problem professional aggregators do: a low-resolution screenshot of a screenshot degrades fast, and a blurry image gets blurrier every time it's re-compressed by a platform's upload pipeline. If you've actually got an original photo — a genuine sighting, a photo from outside a stadium, anything you shot yourself during the window — [compressing it properly](/compress) before you post it keeps it sharp through however many re-shares it picks up, instead of turning into unreadable static by the third repost.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you verify a "spotted at the airport" transfer photo is real?

Run a reverse image search first — a large share of viral "spotted" photos are recycled from a previous transfer window involving a different player. Then check whether the photo has a visible timestamp or metadata consistent with the claimed date; a surprising number of these photos are stock airport images with no connection to the player at all.

### Why do transfer rumor photos look so much worse than official announcement photos?

Rumor photos are almost always shot by a bystander on a phone from a distance, in low light, through glass or a car window — nobody involved wants to be photographed clearly. Official announcement photos are shot by the club's own media team on professional equipment in controlled lighting, which is why the jump in quality between "leaked" and "confirmed" is so dramatic.

## The Takeaway

- A "spotted" transfer photo is the least reliable image in the whole rumor cycle — reverse-search it before you believe or share it.
- Once a deal is official, every leaked photo gets replaced by the club's own polished three-shot sequence within minutes.
- If you've got a genuine photo of your own from a transfer sighting or a signing day, [compress it](/compress) before it gets passed through five rounds of platform re-uploads and turns to mush.
