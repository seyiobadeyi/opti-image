**Storm and hail damage claims get denied or reduced more often over weak documentation than over disputes about whether the damage happened at all.** An adjuster working from three blurry, wide-angle photos has a much harder time approving a full claim than one looking at a documented set that shows the damage from multiple angles, at full resolution, taken before anyone touched the property. With hail and wind damage reports still coming in from storms across the Midwest and South this summer, this is worth getting right the first time — you don't get a second chance to photograph damage that's already been repaired.

## Photograph Before Anyone Touches Anything

The order matters as much as the photos themselves. Document the damage in its original state before any temporary repairs — tarping a roof, boarding a broken window — and definitely before a contractor starts real repair work. Temporary fixes to prevent further damage are the right call and most policies expect them, but photograph first. A photo of a roof after it's already tarped documents the tarp, not the hole underneath it.

This is also the moment fraudulent contractors tend to show up. Storm-chasing crews that canvass neighborhoods right after a hailstorm, promise guaranteed insurance approval, and pressure a same-day signature are a documented pattern every storm season. Photographing the damage yourself, on your own timeline, before anyone else is involved, protects you regardless of who you end up hiring.

## The Wide-Shot-Plus-Close-Up Pattern

Every piece of damage needs two photos, not one. A wide shot establishes context — which side of the roof, which room, which section of siding. A close-up shows the actual damage clearly enough that someone who's never seen your property can tell exactly what happened. A single photo trying to do both jobs usually does neither well: either the damage is too small in frame to assess, or you're so close there's no context for where on the property it is.

1. **Wide establishing shot** — the whole affected section: the roof plane, the fence line, the full window, from a distance that shows where the damage sits.
2. **Close-up of the damage itself** — filling the frame, in focus, with enough light to actually see the extent of it.
3. **A reference object for scale where it helps** — a tape measure or a common object next to hail dents or cracks makes size unambiguous.
4. **Repeat for every distinct area of damage** — don't assume one roof photo covers the whole roof if damage is scattered across multiple sections.

## Keep Originals, Not Forwards

The photos you submit should be your own originals, not a copy that's been through three rounds of texting or a screenshot of a photo someone else sent you. Every re-send through a messaging app re-compresses the image and can strip the metadata — timestamp, and location where available — that helps establish when and where a photo was taken. That metadata isn't the only thing an adjuster considers, but a claim built on your own clean, unmodified files documents your case better than one built on forwards of forwards.

If your originals are large RAW or high-resolution files and you need a lighter set to actually email or upload to your insurer's portal, [compress them in bulk](/compress) rather than letting your phone's messaging app do it inconsistently one photo at a time — you keep control over quality and keep an original archive copy either way.

| Documentation step | What it protects against |
|---|---|
| Photograph before repairs or tarping | Claim getting denied for insufficient evidence of original damage |
| Wide + close-up pairing per damage point | Adjuster unable to locate or assess the damage from the photo alone |
| Keep original files, not forwards | Lost metadata and quality from repeated re-compression |
| Photograph before signing with any contractor | Protection if a canvassing contractor turns out to be unlicensed or fraudulent |
| Date-stamp your own notes alongside photos | A written record that matches your photo timeline |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How many photos should I take for a storm damage claim?**
A: More than feels necessary. Every distinct area of damage gets its own wide-plus-close-up pair — for a full roof with scattered hail damage, that can easily mean 20-30 photos. Adjusters work from what you document; gaps in coverage become gaps in the claim.

**Q: Can I use my phone, or do I need a professional camera?**
A: A modern phone camera is fine for insurance documentation as long as the photos are in focus, well-lit, and show the damage clearly. What matters more than the camera is the process — wide-plus-close-up, before repairs, originals kept intact.

**Q: What if I already let a contractor make emergency repairs before I photographed everything?**
A: Photograph what's still visible immediately, and note in writing what was already addressed and why (preventing further damage is a legitimate reason). It's not ideal, but a partial documented record beats no record, and most adjusters understand emergency mitigation happens before a full damage survey is possible.

## The Takeaway

- Document damage before any repairs, temporary or permanent — this is the single biggest factor in claim documentation
- Pair every wide shot with a close-up, and repeat for every distinct area of damage
- Keep your original files rather than relying on forwarded or re-compressed copies, and compress in bulk yourself when you need a lighter set to submit
