**Two-thirds of back-to-school shoppers had already started buying by early July, according to NRF's 2026 survey, and 55% of them are shopping online before anywhere else.** That's the number that should reset how sellers think about timing this season. The old assumption — that back-to-school really kicks off in the last two weeks of August — is already wrong for most of the market. NRF projects $39.4 billion in K-12 spending and $88.8 billion in back-to-college spending this year, and most of the shoppers behind those numbers are comparing listings online right now, this week, not three weeks from now.

## The Surge Is Already Running

Online has pulled ahead of every other channel for back-to-school shopping — 55% of shoppers start there, ahead of department stores, discount stores, and clothing stores. That's a meaningful shift from a category that used to be dominated by big-box back-to-school displays. It also means the sellers actually capturing this spending are the ones whose listings load fast, look sharp on a phone screen, and hold up against three or four competing tabs open in the same browsing session — not the ones with the best in-store shelf placement.

## The Priority Order, Not the Full Rebuild

Sellers don't need to reshoot their whole catalog this week — they need to fix the handful of things that actually cost conversions during a high-intent comparison-shopping window. In order of impact:

1. **Cut your main listing image file size first.** This is the single highest-leverage fix and the fastest to do across an entire catalog. A 6-8MB camera-original main image adds real load time on mobile, where most back-to-school comparison shopping actually happens. Compressed to a reasonable target — usually under 300KB with no visible quality loss — the same image loads close to instantly.
2. **Check your aspect ratio consistency across a listing.** A gallery where one photo is square, the next is portrait, and the next is an odd crop reads as unprofessional at a glance, and shoppers moving fast between tabs notice inconsistency before they notice detail.
3. **Re-export anything still sitting as a PNG.** Product photography saved as PNG by default (common from certain editing tools) produces needlessly large files for photographic content. Converting to JPEG at high quality usually cuts the file size dramatically with no visible difference.
4. **Batch-process by category, not item by item.** If you're managing more than a handful of listings, doing this one photo at a time is how the fix never actually gets finished before the surge passes. Bulk compression across a folder takes the same total effort as doing five images, not five times the effort.

## Why "Later This Month" Is Already Too Late

The instinct to treat back-to-school as a late-August event is exactly what NRF's numbers are pushing back against. Two-thirds of the spending pool has already started shopping. Every day a listing sits with an unnecessarily slow-loading photo is a day of comparison shopping happening against faster, cleaner competitor listings — and once a shopper buys the notebook set or the backpack from someone else, that sale doesn't come back around for a re-shot photo in three weeks.

The fix here isn't creative, it's mechanical: get the file sizes down, get the formats consistent, and do it across the whole catalog at once rather than picking a few flagship listings.
