**Photographers aren't just complaining about Adobe's pricing anymore — in 2026, the specific complaint is that Lightroom's export and web-sharing pipeline quietly changes their files.** One widely-shared account describes a 7MB JPEG uploaded to a Lightroom web album coming back as a 1.7MB download for the recipient — a real, visible quality cut, applied somewhere between upload and download, with no obvious setting that explains it. Combine that with reports of colour shifts, over-saturation, and export performance that hasn't kept pace with file sizes from modern cameras, and you get a year where "leaving Adobe" stopped being a pricing complaint and became a workflow one.

## The Complaint Isn't New, But 2026 Made It Specific

Subscription fatigue with Adobe has been a low simmer for years. What's different in 2026 is that photographers are naming the exact step that's failing them: export. Not the editing tools, not the organizing catalog — the moment a finished photo leaves Lightroom and becomes a file a client or a website actually receives. Reports describe grain appearing that wasn't in the edit, colours shifting between the preview and the exported file, and — most concretely — file sizes coming out smaller than expected with no visible setting change to explain it.

That last one matters most for anyone delivering photos professionally. A client who receives a visibly softer image than what they saw in the gallery preview doesn't care whose fault it is in the pipeline. They just see a worse photo than the one they picked.

## Where Photographers Are Actually Going

Capture One has been the professional alternative for colour-critical work for years, and its reputation on that front hasn't changed — tethered shooting and colour science are still its strongest draws. What's newer is Affinity Photo, which Canva made free in 2025 after acquiring it, and which now covers most of what a working photographer needs for editing at no cost. Neither move solves the export problem on its own, though — export is a separate step in every one of these tools, and it's worth treating it as one.

## Why the Export Step Deserves Its Own Tool

The core issue with any editor's built-in export or web-sharing feature is that it bundles two different jobs into one black box: rendering your final edit, and then compressing it for delivery. When something goes wrong, you can't tell which step did it. Separating those two jobs fixes that. Edit and finish the photo in whatever tool you're using — Lightroom, Capture One, Affinity, doesn't matter — then hand the finished file to a dedicated compressor where you can see and control exactly what quality level and file size you're getting.

1. **Export your finished edit at full quality first.** Don't let the editing software's export dialog also make your compression decision. Get the highest-quality version out.
2. **Run the batch through a separate compressor with a visible quality setting.** You want to see the before-and-after file size and be able to adjust the quality slider yourself, not trust a preset you didn't choose.
3. **Check one file against the original before sending the whole batch.** A thirty-second spot check catches a bad setting before it goes out to a client, instead of after they've already noticed.

## This Isn't About Picking a Side in the Adobe Debate

Whether you stick with Lightroom, jump to Capture One, or try Affinity Photo's free tier doesn't actually change the underlying problem: photo editors are built to edit, not to give you transparent, predictable control over a final compressed file size. That's a separate job, and treating it as one — rather than trusting whichever export dialog happens to ship with your editor — is the fix that survives whatever software decision you make next.
