**Cody Bellinger drove in two runs in the first inning on his way to MVP honors as the American League shut out the National League 4-0 at Citizens Bank Park on July 15.** Ben Rice added a run, Miguel Vargas capped it with a 433-foot home run in the eighth, and AL pitching turned in one of the most dominant outings in All-Star Game history — 10 of 11 pitchers struck out at least one batter. It was a good night to be at the ballpark with a camera. For most people, it was also a night of photos that came back orange, blurry, or both.

Night baseball is one of the least forgiving environments for a phone camera. The lighting is mixed, the subject moves fast, and you're usually thirty to eighty feet from the play. None of that is fixable by tapping the screen and hoping.

## Why Stadium Photos Come Out Wrong

Stadium floodlights aren't a single, consistent light source. Different fixtures around the park run at different color temperatures, and your phone's auto white balance is guessing at an average across all of them. That guess is usually wrong, which is why night game photos frequently come back with an orange or sickly green tint that doesn't match what your eyes saw live.

The second problem is motion. In low light, a phone's camera app automatically slows the shutter to let in more light, which is exactly the wrong trade for a 95 mph fastball or a bat swing. A slow shutter turns fast motion into a streak instead of a frozen moment. This is the same reason people's photos of Vargas's eighth-inning home run mostly show a blurred bat and a sharp stadium — the camera prioritized brightness over freezing the action.

## The Settings That Actually Fix This

1. **Lock exposure and focus on the field before the pitch, not during it.** Tap and hold on the pitcher's mound or the batter's box a beat before the play starts. This stops the camera from re-guessing exposure mid-swing, which is where a lot of blur and color shift comes from.
2. **Turn off Night Mode for anything with motion.** Save it for a static shot of the stadium bowl or the scoreboard between innings. For the play itself, you want the fastest shutter your phone can manage, not a multi-second exposure.
3. **Shoot burst instead of a single frame.** Hold the shutter button through the swing or the pitch and pick the sharpest frame afterward. One frame out of fifteen will be in focus; the other fourteen are the price of admission.
4. **Correct white balance after the fact, not before.** Fighting the in-camera auto white balance in real time wastes the moment. Shoot, then do a quick warm-to-neutral correction in your photo app before you share — it's a five-second fix on a photo you already have, versus missing the shot trying to dial in settings live.
5. **Get physically closer when you can.** Zoom on a phone camera is digital past 2-3x on most models, and digital zoom in low light is where quality falls apart fastest. A few rows closer beats zooming in every time.

## Burst Mode Fixes the Blur, Then Bloats Your Camera Roll

Here's the part nobody warns you about: a 20-frame burst to catch one good swing is 20 full-resolution photos sitting on your phone, most of them useless. Live Photos are worse — each one is effectively a short video clip disguised as a still, and it adds up fast across a nine-inning game with a decent camera roll habit. By the time you're home from the ballpark, you might have 300-400MB of night game photos for the six or seven shots you'll actually keep and share.

The fix is the same one that applies after any burst-heavy shoot: pick your keepers, delete the rest, and compress before you post or upload to a shared album. A stadium photo destined for a group chat or a Story doesn't need to carry its full 12-48MP burst-mode weight — resizing to something reasonable and running it through a proper compression pass keeps the sharp frame you fought for without dragging the file size that comes from a slow shutter and a high-ISO night shot.

[Optimage's compressor](/compress) handles that in the browser, free, whether you're cleaning up one photo or a whole night's worth of burst shots before they go in a shared gallery.

## What to Do With It

Cody Bellinger's MVP night and Vargas's eighth-inning shot are the kind of games people actually go back and look at their photos from months later — assuming those photos are sharp enough to be worth looking at. Lock exposure before the play, shoot burst not single-frame, skip Night Mode for anything moving, and compress the keepers before they go anywhere. That's the difference between a phone full of orange blur and a handful of shots worth keeping.
