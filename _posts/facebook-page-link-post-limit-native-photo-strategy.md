**Meta is testing something that should worry anyone whose Facebook strategy is "post a link, hope for clicks":** a cap limiting some non-Meta-Verified business Pages to as few as two external link posts a month. It hasn't been announced with a press release — it's showing up as a restriction some Page admins are hitting mid-scroll, with no warning beforehand. The intent is obvious even without a statement: Meta wants people staying inside the Facebook feed, not clicking out to somebody's blog, Shopify store, or news site.

## The Quiet Test That Changes How Pages Have to Post

For years, Facebook's algorithm has already down-ranked link posts relative to native photo and video content — this isn't new. What's new is turning that soft preference into a hard limit for a subset of accounts. If your Page has been posting "new blog post, click here" three times a week, some version of that workflow is about to stop working, full stop, not just underperform.

The Pages most exposed are the ones that never built a native-content habit: publishers who post a headline photo and a link, e-commerce accounts that post a product shot with a "shop now" link, event promoters who post a flyer image with a ticket link. All of that content still has a photo in it already — the fix isn't starting from zero, it's restructuring what the photo is doing in the post.

## Native Photo and Carousel Posts Are the Actual Answer

The workaround Meta is functionally forcing is the one good social managers already knew was coming: **post the photo natively, put context in the caption, and move the link to the first comment or the Page bio.** Facebook's own distribution rules already favor this — a native carousel of 4-6 product photos with a caption reaches more of your existing audience than the same photos wrapped around a single outbound link ever did.

This isn't a downgrade if you do it right. A well-shot native carousel keeps someone in-feed longer, which the algorithm rewards with more reach on your next post too. The catch is that native posts live or die entirely on the image quality, because there's no landing page doing any of the persuading anymore — the photo has to carry the whole message.

## Who Actually Gets Hurt by This, and Who Doesn't

The Pages least affected are the ones that already run a strong native-content calendar — a boutique posting daily product carousels, a restaurant posting plated dishes, a gym posting client transformation photos. None of those accounts were leaning on link posts in the first place, so a cap on something they weren't doing anyway is a non-event.

The Pages actually exposed are a specific type: news and blog publishers whose entire Facebook presence is "headline photo plus link," and small businesses running a funnel where Facebook exists purely to push traffic to a Shopify checkout or a booking page. For both of those, this test isn't a minor annoyance — it's a forced rebuild of how the Page communicates at all, on a timeline Meta didn't announce and isn't obligated to give warning about if the test becomes permanent.

There's also a two-tier outcome worth naming directly: Meta Verified subscribers are reportedly exempt. That means a cap framed as an anti-spam or engagement measure is also, functionally, a paid feature unlock. Whether that's a fair trade depends entirely on how much traffic a given Page actually drives through Facebook links versus everywhere else — for some businesses, the Meta Verified subscription cost is trivial next to what even a few extra link posts a month are worth; for others, it's a subscription for a feature they'll rarely use once native posting becomes the default habit.

## Preparing Photos for a Feed That Compresses Everything Anyway

Facebook re-encodes every image you upload — there's no way around that. But a photo that starts sharp and correctly sized survives that re-encoding far better than one that was already soft or oversized:

- **Export at 2048px on the long edge before upload.** Anything wider just gets downscaled server-side, and anything much smaller forces Facebook to upscale, which introduces softness.
- **Use JPEG, not PNG, for photography.** PNG is lossless but produces huge files for photos; Facebook will re-compress it as JPEG anyway, so you're not preserving anything by uploading PNG.
- **Compress before upload, not after.** A pre-compressed, correctly sized JPEG at quality 85-90 gives Facebook's compression pass a clean source to work from, rather than stacking two rounds of lossy compression on an already-heavy file.
- **Keep carousel images consistent in aspect ratio.** Facebook crops inconsistently sized carousel images to match the first slide, which can cut off product shots or text overlays unpredictably.

## The Longer-Term Bet Meta Is Making

Step back from the specific cap and the pattern is bigger than Facebook: every major platform has spent the last few years slowly closing the door on sending users elsewhere. YouTube discourages off-platform links in the same way, TikTok's algorithm has never rewarded outbound links, and Instagram has treated a bio link as the only acceptable exit for years. Facebook testing a hard numeric cap is just the most explicit version yet of a strategy every platform is already running quietly.

That's a structural fact worth planning around rather than resenting. A business that builds its content strategy assuming native, in-platform engagement is the default — and treats an outbound click as a bonus rather than the whole point — isn't caught off guard when a platform tightens the rules further. A business that's built its entire funnel around Facebook sending traffic to a checkout page is the one that gets hurt every time one of these caps lands, whether the specific mechanism is a link limit, an algorithm downrank, or something else next year.
