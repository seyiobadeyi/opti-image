**A sustained heat wave has HVAC companies logging six to seven service calls per technician a day, roughly double a normal week, and it's the older units — many over 20 years old — failing fastest as capacitors, fan motors, and circuit boards give out under the strain.** When call volume triples, the first thing that gets rushed on a job site is documentation. That's exactly backwards, because a rushed photo — or no photo at all — is what turns a routine repair into a disputed warranty claim weeks later.

## Why This Heat Wave Is Different From a Normal Busy Week

Air conditioning systems are engineered to run hard, but not to run continuously for days without the maintenance downtime they'd normally get during more moderate stretches. When temperatures stay elevated for an extended period, units simply don't get the rest cycles they're designed around, and components start failing in a predictable order: capacitors first, since they're the part most directly stressed by constant cycling, followed by fan motors and circuit boards as heat compounds the mechanical strain. Older systems fail disproportionately because the parts wearing out are harder to source — a 20-year-old unit's capacitor isn't sitting on a shelf at every supply house the way a current model's is.

## What a Before-and-After Photo Actually Settles

A written service note says a capacitor was "failed" or "swollen." A photo shows exactly how swollen, exactly which terminal was corroded, and exactly what the wiring looked like before anyone touched it — evidence that holds up whether the dispute is with a manufacturer's warranty department, a home warranty company, or a homeowner questioning whether a part really needed replacing. When a technician is running double their normal call volume, the temptation is to skip this step entirely and move to the next house. That's the exact week it matters most, because a disputed claim from a rushed week is far more likely than one from a slow week when there was time to be careful.

1. **Photograph the failed component before removing anything**, in place, with enough surrounding context — the unit's model plate, the panel it came from — that the photo is unambiguous about what it shows.
2. **Take the after photo from the same angle and distance as the before photo.** A matched pair is far more persuasive than two photos that don't visually correspond to each other.
3. **Include a timestamp or the day's job ticket in frame if your workflow supports it**, so the photo pair is self-documenting without depending on file metadata that can get stripped by whatever app you used to send it.
4. **Don't rely on a single wide shot.** A wide shot showing the whole unit and a close shot showing the specific failed part together tell a more complete story than either alone.

## Handling the Volume Without Losing the Discipline

The real risk during a stretch like this isn't forgetting the concept of before-and-after photos — every tech already knows to take them. It's the accumulation: 300 service calls in a week means potentially 600+ photos moving through a phone, a shared drive, or a dispatch app, and disorganized volume is what actually causes documentation to fail when it's needed months later for a warranty dispute. A photo that exists but can't be found or has degraded into an unreadable, over-compressed thumbnail is functionally the same as no photo at all.
