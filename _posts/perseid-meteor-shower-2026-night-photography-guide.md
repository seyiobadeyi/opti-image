**The 2026 Perseid meteor shower peaks the night of August 12 into August 13, and this year it happens under a new moon — meaning no moonlight to wash out the sky, and the best viewing conditions the Perseids have had in years.** Under dark skies, observers can expect 50 to 75 meteors an hour, building toward pre-dawn hours. That's a rare enough combination of timing and darkness that it's worth planning around, and it's exactly the kind of shot that a careless export can quietly ruin without the photographer noticing until it's too late to reshoot.

## Why This Year's Conditions Actually Matter

Meteor showers happen every year, but moonlight is the variable that determines whether a given year's Perseids are worth the effort. A bright moon near peak night washes out all but the brightest meteors, turning what should be a 50-75-per-hour show into a handful of visible streaks. A new moon during peak, which is exactly what's happening the night of August 12-13, 2026, removes that interference entirely. The shower is active from July 17 through August 24, so there's a wide window to try, but the peak night under a dark sky is the one worth prioritizing.

## Why Compression Hits Night Photos Harder Than Anything Else You Shoot

Most photos survive moderate compression fine because they have strong edges and high contrast for the algorithm to preserve — a face, a building, clear boundaries between light and dark. A night-sky photo is almost the opposite: its entire value is in low-contrast, subtle gradient detail — the faint structure of the Milky Way, a meteor's dim trailing edge, stars barely above the noise floor of the sensor. Lossy compression algorithms are specifically built to discard the kind of information human vision is least sensitive to, and that's disproportionately the low-contrast detail a night photo depends on. The same compression setting that looks fine on a daytime landscape can visibly crush a night sky into blotchy, banded darkness with the faintest meteor trails gone entirely.

## Shooting and Exporting for the Peak Night

1. **Shoot RAW if your camera or phone supports it.** RAW preserves the full sensor data, including the faint gradient detail a compressed JPEG discards at the moment of capture, before you've even had a chance to edit it.
2. **Use a tripod and a long exposure, not a handheld night mode shot.** Meteor trails need several seconds of exposure to register at all; a quick handheld shot won't capture the trail even before compression becomes a factor.
3. **Get away from ambient light**, ideally somewhere with minimal light pollution — the new moon removes one source of sky brightness, but city and streetlight glow will still wash out the fainter meteors and stars regardless of moon phase.
4. **Export at high quality, low compression, when you process the final image.** A night-sky shot is the wrong place to save file size at the expense of quality — the detail that makes the photo worth taking is exactly what a lower-quality export sacrifices first.
5. **Compress only after confirming the detail holds up.** If you do need to reduce file size for sharing, check the compressed version at full zoom against the original before posting — banding and lost detail in a night sky are much more visible zoomed in than they first appear at thumbnail size.

## Worth Planning For

A new-moon Perseid peak doesn't happen every year — lining up the shower's peak date with the darkest possible sky is closer to a scheduling coincidence than a guarantee, and next year's peak may well fall closer to a bright moon that washes half the show out. If you're going to make the effort to get somewhere dark on the night of August 12-13, it's worth the extra minute making sure the photo you bring home actually holds the detail you were out there to capture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When do the Perseids peak in 2026 and why are conditions especially good this year?**
The 2026 Perseid meteor shower peaks on the night of August 12 into August 13. A new moon falls during that peak window, meaning no moonlight will wash out the sky, giving observers under dark skies a chance to see meteor rates as high as 50-75 per hour.

**Why do night-sky photos lose more quality to compression than daytime photos?**
A daytime photo has strong contrast and sharp edges that compression algorithms preserve reasonably well. A night-sky photo's meaningful detail — faint stars, a meteor's trail, subtle Milky Way structure — exists in low-contrast, low-light gradients that aggressive compression flattens or discards first, which is why a heavily compressed night photo often looks noticeably worse than the same compression applied to a daytime shot.
