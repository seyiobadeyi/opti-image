**Every contentious call at this year's World Cup got resolved in under a minute, on a stadium screen, in front of everyone watching — and that sentence would have made no sense at any World Cup before 2018.** VAR wasn't a fixture of the tournament; it was a 2018 addition, and the version of it fans watched this July looks almost nothing like the version that debuted in Russia. That's eight years of steady change to what a "big moment" photo at a World Cup even looks like.

## Before 2018: The Call Was the Call

Every World Cup before Russia 2018 ran entirely without video review. A referee's read on an offside, a handball, a penalty shout — right or wrong, in real time, final. That produced its own genre of photograph: the referee pointing to the spot immediately, players surrounding him mid-argument, no pause, no screen, no second look. Some of the sport's most famous controversies — Maradona's 1986 "Hand of God," Frank Lampard's disallowed 2010 goal against Germany — exist as photographs precisely because there was no mechanism to correct them in the moment. The photo of the wrong call became the historical record, not a footnote to a later correction.

## 2018 to 2022: From a Referee at a Monitor to a Computer-Drawn Line

The first VAR era was a referee jogging to the sideline monitor, headset on, watching a replay while 80,000 people waited. Photographically, that produced one dominant image per controversy: the referee's face lit by the monitor, players standing around in various states of frustration or hope. Qatar 2022 changed the visual language entirely. Semi-automated offside meant the stadium screen itself became the graphic everyone looked at — a 3D rendering of the offside line, generated from tracking cameras and a sensor inside the match ball, shown to the crowd in close to real time instead of a referee's private review. The photograph of "the controversial call" stopped being a referee's expression and became a broadcast graphic.

## 2026: The Review Became Part of the Match Photo

This year pushed the same trend further. Connected-ball sensor data fed decisions into the review process fast enough that a chunk of the stoppage-time drama in matches like [the Argentina-England semifinal](/blog/world-cup-2026-argentina-england-semifinal-stoppage-time-photo-guide) played out with the review graphic on screen for the crowd almost as quickly as the incident itself. That's a genuinely new photographic subject: not just the goal, not just the reaction, but the few seconds where a stadium full of people is staring at the same automated line on the same screen, waiting to find out if what they just celebrated actually counted.

## What This Means If You're Archiving Match Photos

If your World Cup photo collection spans more than one tournament, this is worth sorting for on its own: pre-2018 photos will never have a review-screen shot in them, because the category didn't exist. Photos from 2018 onward increasingly do, and by 2026 the review moment is often as widely shared as the goal it's reviewing. Anyone building a photo history across tournaments — 2018, 2022, and this year — is effectively also building a small visual history of officiating technology without meaning to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was VAR first used at a World Cup?

VAR made its World Cup debut in 2018 in Russia. Before that, referees had no video review at all — a contentious call was final the moment it was made, right or wrong, based only on what the officials on the pitch saw in real time.

### What is semi-automated offside technology and when did it start at the World Cup?

Semi-automated offside technology, introduced at the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, uses tracking cameras and a sensor in the ball to generate an automated offside line rather than relying on a VAR official manually drawing lines on broadcast footage. It cut review times significantly and gave stadium screens a cleaner graphic to show fans in real time.

### Why do stadium review-screen photos matter now?

Because the review process itself has become visible to fans in the stadium in a way it wasn't a decade ago — the offside line, the review clock, the players waiting — which means photographers and fans alike are now capturing an entirely new category of match moment that didn't exist before 2018.
