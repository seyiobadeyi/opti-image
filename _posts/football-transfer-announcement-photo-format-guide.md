**Tottenham has spent close to €567 million on six players this summer — Sandro Tonali, Mateus Fernandes, Jan Paul van Hecke, Andrew Robertson, Marcos Senesi, and Martin Dubravka — and every single one of those signings was announced with a photo that looks almost identical to the last one.** Same pose, same lighting, same three-shot sequence. That's not a lack of imagination from club media teams. It's a format that's been refined over a decade of transfer windows because it's the one that actually performs.

## The Three Shots Every Club Uses

Watch any official signing announcement from Chelsea, Manchester City, or Liverpool this summer and you'll see the same sequence every time: the player holding the shirt up to the camera with both hands, a walkout shot onto the pitch alone, and a tighter close-up in the tunnel or dressing room. Chelsea has run this exact formula for Marco Palestra, Geovany Quenda, Emmanuel Emegha, and every other signing under Xabi Alonso this window. Manchester City did the same for Elliot Anderson, their club-record deal for a Nottingham Forest midfielder. The format doesn't vary because clubs have already tested what varies and what doesn't move engagement, and the answer is: almost nothing about the pose matters once you've got these three angles.

A single announcement photo has to work as a 16:9 thumbnail on the club's YouTube channel, a square post on Instagram's main feed, and a 9:16 slide in a Story, often within the same hour of the deal being confirmed. Shooting at 45+ megapixels means the media team crops one source file three different ways instead of arranging three separate shoots — the shirt-hold shot is framed loosely enough on purpose that a tight vertical crop still keeps the player's face and the shirt crest both in frame. That's a compositional decision made specifically to survive a crop nobody's shot yet, and it's the actual reason the pose looks so rigid: any pose that only works at one aspect ratio gets thrown out in review before it's ever posted.

## The Part Nobody Sees: Metadata Stripping

Every one of these files goes through a metadata pass before publishing, and it's not optional. A raw camera file carries GPS coordinates if the camera has location tagging on, plus the camera body's serial number and sometimes lens data. For most photography that's irrelevant. For a club shooting a signing at a training ground the location of which isn't public, GPS metadata embedded in the file is a real disclosure risk — reporters and rival clubs already comb metadata on leaked images looking for exactly this kind of tell. Stripping EXIF before publishing is standard practice at every club with a media operation worth the name, and it happens on every single image in the sequence, not just the ones that look sensitive.

## What This Means If You're Not a Premier League Club

The three-shot habit and the crop-ready framing translate directly to any business announcing new hires, partnerships, or product launches with a photo. Shoot slightly looser than you think you need to, so the same image survives a square crop and a vertical crop without cutting off the subject. And strip metadata before you publish anything shot on a phone — location data embedded in a photo of your storefront or office is exactly the same disclosure risk at a much smaller scale, and it's a one-click fix.
