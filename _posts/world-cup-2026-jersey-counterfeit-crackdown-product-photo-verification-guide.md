**U.S. Customs and Border Protection closed its FIFA World Cup 2026 enforcement campaign on July 20 having made 2,246 seizures — 476,903 counterfeit items with a combined manufacturer's suggested retail value of $33.1 million.** One Miami operation alone stopped 8,400 fake Nike jerseys worth $840,000 at retail price. Houston's airport intercepted almost $9 million in fake merchandise on its own. Separately, Spanish police running Operation CLEANTRADE with EUIPO, Europol, Interpol, and OLAF seized more than 66,000 counterfeit jerseys before a ball was even kicked. A month after the final, the fake-kit economy hasn't stopped — it's just moved from stadium sidewalks to resale apps, where the fight over what's real is being won or lost in the listing photos.

## The Seizure Numbers Are the Tip of What Got Through

Every counterfeit operation large enough to make a customs press release represents thousands more units that customers already unboxed at home. That's the actual scale problem: 476,903 seized items is what got caught crossing a border or moving through a distribution hub before the tournament ended. What's circulating now, a month out, is jerseys that already made it to buyers — some sold knowingly as replicas, plenty sold as something closer to authentic than they actually are. Texas authorities separately seized nearly $114,000 in fake World Cup jerseys in a single operation, and Indianapolis and Mexico-specific seizures (over 18,000 counterfeit Mexico jerseys worth $2.7 million, plus 68,000 fake FIFA mascot toys) show the counterfeiting wasn't limited to one team or one country's kit.

## Why the Fight Moved to Resale Photos

Once the tournament ends, the counterfeit conversation shifts from "is this stadium vendor legit" to "is this eBay/Depop/Vinted listing legit" — and that's a photo problem, not a customs problem. A genuine match-worn or player-issue jersey has specific, hard-to-fake tells: stitched (not heat-pressed) crests, correct sponsor logo placement for the specific match date, tag fonts that match the manufacturing year, and Aeroready or Dri-FIT tagging consistent with the actual retail run. None of that survives a screenshot of a screenshot, and it barely survives an image that's been compressed three times as it bounced between a group chat, a marketplace app, and a listing page.

Serious buyers of high-value kit now routinely ask sellers for the original photo file, not the platform-compressed thumbnail — because metadata and un-degraded detail are two of the few things a counterfeiter selling in bulk won't bother faking for every individual listing. A seller with nothing to hide can produce that file in seconds. One who can't is a signal worth reading.

## What a Credible Resale Listing Actually Needs

1. **Shoot flat, in even light, with the tag and crest both in frame at full resolution.** A single hero shot from a phone at an angle doesn't let a buyer zoom into stitching — and buyers who care about authenticity will try to zoom.
2. **Keep the original file, not a re-save.** Every re-export through a messaging app or a second platform strips detail and often strips metadata. If you're asked to prove a jersey is real, the version that's been resized four times already won't help your case.
3. **Photograph the tag and any hologram/authentication sticker separately, close up.** These are the details counterfeiters get wrong most often, and they're also the details buyers zoom into first.
4. **Compress for the listing, not for the proof.** Upload a properly compressed set for the actual listing page — [Optimage's compress tool](/compress) handles that without degrading the detail a buyer would need to verify — and keep the untouched original on hand separately in case a buyer asks for it directly.
5. **Strip location metadata before sharing broadly, keep everything else.** If you're posting to a public marketplace, [removing GPS metadata](/metadata) protects you without touching the image quality a buyer is checking against.

## This Isn't Unique to Jerseys, But the Scale Is

The same pattern — a defensible seizure number at the border, a much larger shadow economy in resale afterward — plays out with [World Cup ticket resale](/blog/world-cup-2026-final-ticket-resale-verification-photo-guide) and [memorabilia digitization](/blog/world-cup-2026-memorabilia-ticket-jersey-photo-digitization-guide), where the same rule applies: a photo that can't survive a zoom-in isn't proof of anything. What makes jerseys different is volume — 476,903 seized items means millions more are already circulating, and most of them will get sold at least once more before this World Cup cycle is fully over.

## Get Your Own Photos Right the First Time

If you're reselling anything from this tournament — kit, tickets, signed items — the photo you take now is the record you'll be relying on months from now when the next buyer asks for proof. [Compress and organize your listing photos properly](/compress) instead of letting a marketplace app degrade them for you on upload.
