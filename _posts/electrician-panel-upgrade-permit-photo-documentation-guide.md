**Electrical panel upgrades almost always require a permit and a formal inspection, and the electricians who pass on the first visit are consistently the ones with organized, labeled photo documentation ready before the inspector even shows up.** It's a detail that's easy to treat as an afterthought — the wiring is the actual work, after all — but a disorganized or missing photo record is one of the most common reasons a straightforward panel upgrade turns into a second inspection visit.

## Why Documentation Matters as Much as the Wiring Itself

An inspector's job is to verify that work meets code, and a meaningful chunk of that work — conduit runs, wire gauge behind a wall, grounding connections that get covered before final walkthrough — is physically inaccessible by the time the inspection happens. Photo documentation from during the work is often the only way to verify that hidden work correctly, and inspectors increasingly expect it rather than treating it as a nice extra. A contractor who shows up with clear before, during, and after photos, properly labeled, gives the inspector everything needed to sign off quickly. A contractor without that documentation is asking the inspector to either trust verbal description or schedule a follow-up visit before anything gets covered — a delay nobody wants on either side.

There's a second audience for this documentation beyond the inspector: the homeowner. Panel upgrade photos increasingly get requested directly by homeowners for their own records, both for warranty purposes and because a documented electrical upgrade is a genuine selling point during a future home sale — buyers and their inspectors want to see exactly what was done and when.

## A Three-Stage System That Actually Holds Up

1. **Before work starts:** Photograph the existing panel and any wiring being replaced, ideally with circuits labeled if the original panel had legible labeling. This establishes the baseline an inspector or a future homeowner can compare against.
2. **Mid-installation, before anything gets covered:** This is the stage most contractors skip and the one inspectors most want to see. Conduit runs, grounding connections, anything that will be behind drywall or siding by the time of final inspection needs to be photographed while it's still visible.
3. **After completion:** The finished panel with clear, legible circuit labeling, plus a photo of the permit and inspection sticker once issued — this becomes the homeowner's permanent record and the fastest reference point if any question comes up years later.

## The Labeling Discipline That Makes This Actually Useful

Photos taken but never organized are barely better than no photos at all when an inspector needs to find a specific shot fast. Label every file with the job address, the date, and the stage — "142-Maple-panel-before," "142-Maple-conduit-during," "142-Maple-final-completed" — while you're still on-site, not back at the shop trying to remember which job a given photo belongs to. A folder full of IMG_ filenames from three different job sites that day is exactly the situation that turns a five-minute inspection question into a delay.
