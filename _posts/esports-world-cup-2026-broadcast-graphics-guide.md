**The 2026 Esports World Cup is running 25 tournaments across 24 different games in Paris through August 23, with a $75 million total prize pool — the largest in esports and the event's first time held outside Saudi Arabia.** The Honor of Kings World Cup and the Overwatch Champions Series Midseason Championship are both underway within it as of this week, alongside titles like Street Fighter 6. Most tournament coverage focuses on the players and the prize money. The less-visible job is the one facing whoever's producing broadcast graphics for an event that has to look like one cohesive brand across two dozen games that don't share a single visual language.

## One Event, 24 Completely Different Visual Worlds

A single-game tournament's broadcast team designs one overlay system, tuned to one game's colors, one aspect ratio, one visual pace. The Esports World Cup can't do that. A fighting game like Street Fighter 6 runs at a different native resolution and pacing than a MOBA like Honor of Kings, which looks nothing like a tactical shooter's overlay needs. Every one of those 24 titles has its own established visual identity that its competitive community expects to see respected, while the event itself needs a consistent, recognizable graphics package tying the whole thing together as one tournament brand.

## Where This Actually Gets Difficult

The stress point isn't the live broadcast overlay itself — that's designed once per title in advance, with time to get it right. It's everything produced during and after each match: player cards, highlight clip thumbnails, social recap graphics, and daily result summaries that need to go out fast across every one of the 24 concurrent tournaments, each with its own roster of players, its own in-game footage style, and its own community expecting the graphics to feel native to that game rather than a generic template stretched over unfamiliar art.

A highlight thumbnail pulled from Street Fighter 6 footage and one pulled from Honor of Kings footage need completely different crop ratios, color treatment, and text placement to look intentional rather than like the same box awkwardly resized. Doing that well, at the volume a 25-tournament event produces daily, is a production problem most single-game esports coverage never has to solve.

## How Multi-Title Events Actually Keep Up

1. **Build a flexible template system, not one fixed template.** A graphics package with adjustable color, crop, and text zones per title lets a team hit a consistent brand feel without hand-designing a bespoke overlay for all 24 games from scratch.
2. **Standardize the export pipeline even when the visual design varies.** Whatever the final graphic looks like per title, the file format, resolution, and compression settings for delivery should stay consistent across every game so the output pipeline isn't reinvented 24 times.
3. **Pre-build title-specific crop presets for each game's native footage.** Knowing in advance that a fighting game's footage needs a different aspect-ratio crop than a MOBA's saves a decision every single clip would otherwise require during a live event.
4. **Batch-process daily recap graphics by tournament, not by clip.** With multiple tournaments running concurrently, processing a day's output tournament-by-tournament keeps each title's content moving without cross-contaminating crop or color settings between games.

## The Underlying Challenge Isn't Going Away

The Esports World Cup's multi-title model is by design — it's meant to be the Olympics of esports, not a single-game championship, and that structure isn't changing for future editions. Any production team taking on coverage of an event like this, or building content around it after the fact, is going to keep running into the same problem: one brand, two dozen visual languages, and a delivery pipeline that has to handle all of them without slowing down.
