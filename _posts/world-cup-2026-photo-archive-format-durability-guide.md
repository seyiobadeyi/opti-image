**The World Cup's over, your camera roll from a month of matches, fan zones, and screenshots is bursting, and the question worth asking right now isn't how good those photos look today — it's whether you'll be able to open them at all in 2030.** That's a genuinely different problem than photo quality, and it's the one most people never think about until a format they relied on quietly stops being supported.

## Why This Question Actually Matters for a Tournament Archive

Every four years, World Cup photo collections face the same slow-motion risk: the phone that took them gets replaced, the cloud service storing them changes its terms or shuts down a feature, and the format they were saved in either ages fine or doesn't. HEIC — the default capture format on most modern iPhones — compresses efficiently and looks great, but it still isn't opened natively by every piece of software, every printer, or every older device a decade from now might involve. That's not a knock on HEIC as a format; it's a genuinely open question about whether today's near-universal support holds for the next ten years the way JPEG's has for the last thirty.

## Two Different Risks, Not One

The format question and the platform question are separate problems that get conflated constantly. Format obsolescence is about whether the file itself will open on whatever device or software exists years from now, independent of where it's stored. Platform risk is about whether the service holding your only copy — a specific phone's cloud backup, a specific social app's saved-photos feature — still exists, still includes your account, and hasn't changed its terms in a way that limits access. A World Cup photo can survive one of these risks and still fail the other: a JPEG that opens on anything is still lost if the only copy sits in an account you eventually lose access to, and a photo backed up redundantly across three services is still unreadable if it was only ever saved in a format none of them can open properly a decade later.

## What Actually Solves Both Problems

1. **Convert your genuinely "keep forever" World Cup photos to JPEG specifically**, even if they were originally captured in HEIC or another newer format, as a dedicated archival copy — not a replacement for the original, a companion to it.
2. **Store that converted copy somewhere independent of a single phone's ecosystem** — a separate hard drive, a cloud service you control directly rather than one bundled into a device subscription, or both.
3. **Label the archive by year and stage** — "2026 World Cup, Quarterfinals" beats an unsorted folder — because the organizational problem compounds every four years just as much as the format problem does, and future-you sorting through four tournaments' worth of unlabeled files is its own kind of loss.
4. **Don't confuse "shared" with "archived."** A photo you posted to social media is compressed by that platform for delivery, not preserved for permanence — the platform's version and your archival version are different files serving different purposes.

Pick your best 50 or so photos from the last month, convert them to JPEG, drop them in a clearly labeled folder somewhere that isn't tied to a single phone or a single subscription, and stop worrying about whether you'll be able to open them at the next World Cup.
