**Kim Kardashian's new energy drink venture is generating exactly the kind of headline-grabbing buzz a celebrity CPG launch is built to generate — and underneath the marketing noise, it's also a genuinely useful case study in one of the hardest product categories to photograph and compress well: beverages.** A can or bottle shot has to nail condensation, carbonation, reflective surface highlights, and color accuracy all at once, and any one of those falling apart under bad compression makes an otherwise strong product photo look cheap. A launch at this scale generates thousands of images fast — official press kit shots, retailer listing photos, and an avalanche of unboxing and taste-test posts within days — and most of those images are handled by people who've never had to think hard about why a soda can photo is deceptively difficult.

## Why Beverage Shots Break Under Compression Faster Than Most Products

A flat product photo — a shirt, a book, a piece of furniture — mostly involves large areas of consistent color and texture, which compresses efficiently with very little visible quality loss. A beverage shot is close to the opposite case: condensation droplets are small, high-contrast, and scattered irregularly across the surface; carbonation bubbles inside a translucent liquid create fine internal detail; a metallic can reflects its environment in ways that shift subtly across the surface. All of that is exactly the kind of high-frequency, high-contrast detail that aggressive compression algorithms sacrifice first, because it's the "noisiest" part of the image from a data standpoint — even though it's often the most important part of the shot for making a cold drink look appealing.

## The Volume Problem a Celebrity Launch Creates

A launch backed by a name with this kind of reach doesn't generate one hero shot that needs to look good — it generates an entire ecosystem of images within days: the brand's official press kit and retailer listing photos, then a wave of creator unboxings, taste-test content, and fan re-shares that all need to hold up visually even though nobody involved beyond the original brand team controlled the shoot. For the brand side specifically, that means the source images need to survive being resized and re-compressed across a dozen different retailer platforms and social formats without falling apart — which is exactly where beverage photography's fragile detail becomes a real operational problem, not just an aesthetic one.

## Shooting and Compressing a Beverage Product Right

1. **Shoot at the highest practical resolution and compress down per destination**, rather than exporting one mid-quality master and hoping it holds up everywhere. Most retailer and social platforms render well under 2000px wide — [Optimage's resize tool](/resize) lets you generate the right size for each channel from one high-quality source.
2. **Compress conservatively on condensation-heavy shots specifically.** A setting that looks fine on a flat product photo can visibly smear droplet detail on a chilled can — [Optimage's compress tool](/compress) lets you control quality level rather than accepting a platform's automatic, often overly aggressive default.
3. **Use WebP or AVIF for web listing pages** to get real file-size savings without touching the compression level that's actually preserving your product's detail — a format switch buys headroom that quality-cutting doesn't have to spend. [Optimage's convert tool](/convert) handles the format swap in a batch.
4. **Keep an untouched master file of every hero shot.** A launch this size will need re-crops and re-exports for weeks after the initial drop — starting from a degraded, already-compressed file for a new ad format compounds quality loss with every generation.
5. **Standardize the whole campaign's file set before distributing it to retail partners and creators.** A mixed batch of different resolutions and formats sent out to a dozen partners creates inconsistent-looking listings across the exact platforms where a customer is comparing your product to a competitor's side by side.

## Why This Matters Beyond One Celebrity Launch

Every CPG brand entering a crowded beverage category — energy drinks especially, one of the most saturated shelves in the store right now — is competing on shelf appeal that's increasingly a photo, not a physical shelf, given how much beverage discovery now happens through a retailer app or a social feed first. Getting the compression and format choices right isn't a nice-to-have polish step; it's the difference between a product photo that reads as premium and one that reads as an amateur re-upload, at the exact moment a customer is deciding whether to add it to a cart.
