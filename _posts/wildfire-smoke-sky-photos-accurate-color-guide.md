**Smoke from more than 800 wildfires burning across Canada — 188 of them officially out of control — has pushed air quality alerts across Michigan, Wisconsin, New York, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Maryland, New Jersey, and Delaware this week, with roughly 100 million people exposed to hazardous air.** Detroit, Minneapolis, and Chicago ranked among the most polluted major cities in the world on Thursday. It's the kind of sky that makes people reach for their phones — and the kind of sky most phone cameras are specifically engineered to correct.

If your smoke photos have come out looking like an ordinary overcast afternoon instead of the genuinely strange, orange-tinted haze outside your window, that's not bad luck. That's your camera doing exactly what it's designed to do, which happens to be the wrong thing for this particular shot.

## Why Your Camera Is Working Against You

Auto white balance exists to make photos look normal under whatever lighting they were shot in — it identifies what should be neutral gray or white in a scene and adjusts every other color relative to that. Wildfire smoke scatters and filters sunlight in a way that shifts everything toward orange and yellow, and your phone reads that shift as an error to correct rather than a real condition to preserve. The result is a photo that's been quietly color-corrected back toward "normal," which defeats the entire reason you took it.

HDR does something similar with contrast and brightness. A smoke-choked sky often has a strange, flattened quality in person — the sun visible as a dim orange disc, the whole sky one dull color instead of the usual gradient from bright horizon to deep blue overhead. HDR processing is built to expand dynamic range and produce a more "balanced" image, which smooths over exactly the flat, ominous quality that makes these skies look the way they actually look.

## How to Actually Capture It

1. **Switch to a manual or Pro shooting mode if your phone has one.** This gives you direct control over white balance instead of leaving it to the auto-correction algorithm, which is the single biggest factor in getting an accurate-looking smoke photo.
2. **Set white balance to daylight or a fixed Kelvin value manually**, rather than auto. This stops the phone from neutralizing the orange cast that's the actual subject of the photo.
3. **Turn off HDR or Smart HDR for this specific shot.** A single, un-blended exposure preserves the flat, dim quality of a smoke-filtered sky better than a processed composite does.
4. **Expose for the sky, not the ground.** Tap on the brightest part of the sky to set exposure there — this keeps the sun's dim orange disc and the sky's color from getting blown out or auto-brightened past what it actually looked like.
5. **Shoot the same framing at different points in the day if you're documenting change.** Smoke density and sky color shift over hours; a consistent angle from a window or doorway across a few shots tells the actual story better than one photo.

## Documenting a Multi-Day Event Without Filling Up Your Phone

Smoke events like this one don't resolve in a single afternoon — this wave has been building since earlier in the week and is forecast to persist as long as the source fires keep burning. If you're taking a photo from the same window every few hours to track how the sky changes, that adds up to a real number of images by the time it clears, especially if you're also sharing them to a group chat or social feed as it develops.

The same rule that applies to any documentation series applies here: keep your full-quality originals if you want an accurate record, but compress the versions you're actually sending or posting. A phone photo shot in Pro mode at full resolution doesn't need to travel through a group chat at 8-12MB per image, and a compressed version loses nothing meaningful for how it's actually going to be viewed — on a phone screen, in a feed, at a glance.

[Optimage](/compress) handles that resizing and compression for free, in the browser, whether it's one photo or a whole series tracking the sky across a day.

## What to Actually Do

Check your local air quality alert for the real guidance on outdoor exposure — that's not something a photography article should be the authority on. For the photo itself: switch off auto white balance and HDR, expose for the sky, and shoot from wherever you already are rather than extending time outside for the shot. The sky this week is genuinely unusual. It's worth a photo that actually looks like it.
