**Club media teams are shooting squad photos in installments this July, not in one sitting, because World Cup finalists are still coming back from a tournament that ended nine days ago.** Federico Valverde returned to Real Madrid training after 24 days off. Rodri won't appear in a squad photo at all for months — he needs back surgery and will miss the start of Manchester City's season. Alexis Mac Allister and Víctor Muñoz, both finalists, are guaranteed at least three weeks off under FIFA rules, which puts them right up against an August 23 opener. For every club with a World Cup finalist on the roster, media day isn't a single event this year. It's a rolling one.

## Why Preseason Photo Day Is Staggered This Year

Most preseasons work the same way every year: the whole squad reports on roughly the same date, the media team runs one kit launch shoot, one set of headshots, one squad photo, and moves on. This year that model breaks down for any club with players who went deep at the World Cup. Everton's preseason began July 10. Many other Premier League clubs followed on July 13. But FIFA's post-tournament rest rule means finalists aren't obligated to report until at least three weeks after July 19 — August 9 at the earliest — and clubs can't force an earlier return.

The result is a squad photo that can't actually be a squad photo yet. A club shoots what it has in mid-July, then reshoots individual portraits and kit shots as each returning international walks back through the door across late July and August. Add new signings doing their own unveiling shoots on top of returning players, and a media officer is running a photo session every few days for a month instead of one big day.

Valverde is the clearest example of an early-back finalist-adjacent case: Uruguay's run ended before the final, so he's already through a full training block at Valdebebas and available for squad content now. Rodri sits at the other extreme — City's media team simply won't have a current photo of him in training kit for a while, and press materials will need to account for a player who's absent for reasons that have nothing to do with scheduling. Mac Allister and Muñoz represent the hardest case for a media officer to plan around: both are entitled to rest past August 9, and Spain's, Argentina's, and their clubs' preseason friendly and press schedules are all trying to fit around a player who legally doesn't have to show up yet, with a season opener bearing down regardless.

## What This Does to a Media Team's Actual Workload

Every club in Europe runs the same handful of image types through preseason: squad photos, individual player portraits in home and away kit, kit launch campaign shots, staff photos, and press-day headshots for interviews. Normally that's one concentrated shoot. This year, a club with two or three deep World Cup runners is shooting those same categories two or three separate times as players filter in — plus doing it again for every new signing's unveiling. A media officer who'd normally process one 300-photo batch and be done is instead processing five smaller batches spread across five weeks, each one needing the same resizing, compression, and delivery steps as the last.

That's not a creative problem. It's a volume-and-repetition problem, and it's exactly the kind of task that eats a media team's time in July when everyone would rather be at the training ground than at a desk.

| Task | Single preseason shoot (normal year) | Staggered return (this year) |
|---|---|---|
| Squad photo sessions | 1 | 3-5, as players return |
| Kit launch shoots | 1 batch | Repeated per late arrival + new signings |
| Photos needing compression before upload | ~300 at once | ~300 total, in 5 smaller uploads |
| Press-day headshot sets | 1 | 1 per returning group |
| Gallery links sent to press/marketing | 1 | Multiple, spaced over weeks |

## Handling the Rush Without Falling Behind

The practical fix isn't shooting less — a media officer doesn't control when Mac Allister lands. It's not letting each small batch become its own manual chore. Every returning player produces another folder of full-resolution RAW-derived images that need to shrink down for web and app use before anyone can publish them, and doing that one photo at a time five separate times in a month is where hours disappear.

1. **Batch-compress each return session as it happens** rather than waiting to combine everything at the end — waiting means a backlog builds right as the season opener approaches.
2. **Keep file sizes consistent across sessions.** A squad page with photos compressed at wildly different settings across July and August looks inconsistent even if every individual shot is fine.
3. **Deliver galleries to marketing and press as a link, not an email chain of attachments.** A club sending five separate rounds of kit shots benefits from one shareable gallery per session that press and marketing can pull from directly.
4. **Don't wait for the full squad to be assembled before processing what's already shot.** Valverde's portraits don't need to sit unprocessed until Mac Allister arrives — process and deliver in the order players actually return.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long do World Cup finalists get off before returning to their club?**
FIFA rules guarantee finalists a minimum of three weeks off after the tournament final. Since the 2026 final was played July 19, players who reached the final are entitled to rest until at least August 9, which puts an August 23 league opener within reach but tight.

**Why are some players back at preseason training already while others aren't?**
Return dates track how far a player's country went in the tournament. Players eliminated in the group stage or early knockout rounds have been back for weeks, while semifinalists and finalists are still working through their mandated rest period and arrive closer to the season opener.

**What's the biggest photo workload for club media teams right now?**
Processing squad photos, kit launch shoots, and press-day headshots for players who are all arriving on different dates rather than as one group, which means repeated small photo sessions instead of a single bulk shoot.
