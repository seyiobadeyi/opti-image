More than 185 million Americans were under heat alerts this past holiday weekend, with heat index values hitting 115°F across the eastern US and at least 39 daily temperature records broken between Thursday and Friday. Philadelphia and Washington DC both approached all-time record highs. New York's Central Park hit 100°F for the first time since 2012.

For anyone running an HVAC business, this is the week AC units that have limped along for three summers finally give out — and the phone doesn't stop ringing. Call volume during a heat dome like this routinely runs double or triple normal summer pace. Which means less time per job, more jobs per day, and documentation being the first thing that gets skipped when you're racing to the next emergency call.

Don't skip it. Here are the five photos worth taking on every single job, and what to do about the storage problem they create.

## The 5 Photos That Matter

**1. The nameplate.** Model number, serial number, manufacture date — one photo, timestamped by your phone, before you do anything else. This is your reference if a parts order needs the exact spec, and it's your proof of which unit you actually worked on if a dispute ever comes up.

**2. Condition on arrival.** Before you touch the unit, one wide shot showing its state — dirty coils, obvious corrosion, a bent fan blade, whatever's visible. This protects you from being blamed for pre-existing damage you didn't cause.

**3. The actual failure point.** A burst run capacitor, a refrigerant leak stain on a line set, a corroded coil, a seized compressor. Close, clear, in focus. This is the single most important photo for a warranty claim — manufacturers increasingly require photo evidence of the specific failure mode before they'll honor a part replacement, and "I told them what I saw" doesn't hold up the way a photo does.

**4. Completed repair.** The same angle as photo #2, now fixed. This is your proof of work and it's the photo customers actually want to see if they ask "what did you actually do."

**5. Final reading.** Gauges showing correct refrigerant pressure, or the thermostat showing the unit is now pulling the house down to temperature. This is what closes the loop — proof the system works, not just proof you touched it.

## The Problem This Creates: Your Phone Fills Up Mid-Heat-Wave

Do the math on a busy day. Ten emergency calls, five photos each, at a typical 3-8MB per photo from a modern phone camera, and you're generating 150-400MB of new photos before lunch. Across a two-week heat event that doesn't let up, that's multiple gigabytes sitting on the same phone you're using to run your dispatch app, process payments, and take the next call. Storage warnings start popping up exactly when you have zero time to deal with them, and the instinct to just delete old photos to make room is exactly how you lose documentation you might need for a dispute three months later.

The fix isn't a bigger phone plan or manually deleting things under pressure — it's compressing photos before they go anywhere permanent. A [bulk image compressor](/compress) run on the day's photos (batch, not one at a time) takes a folder of 3-8MB originals down to 400KB-1MB WebP files with no loss of documentation value — you can still read a serial number or see a corroded coil clearly at that size. That's a 70-80% storage reduction that costs you nothing in terms of what the photos actually prove.

If you're uploading to a CRM, invoicing platform, or a shared client folder, doing this compression pass before upload also means faster uploads over spotty job-site Wi-Fi or cellular data — which matters when you're trying to close out an invoice and move to the next call, not stare at an upload progress bar in 100-degree heat.

## Build the Habit Now, Not During the Next Heat Wave

The contractors who benefit most from a system like this are the ones who set it up when they're not already slammed. Pick a phone folder or app where all job photos land automatically, run a nightly or twice-daily compression batch, and back up to wherever you store client records. It takes five minutes to set up and saves you from the exact situation described above the next time a heat dome sends your call volume through the roof.

## Insurance and Warranty Claims Move Faster With Photos, Not Slower

There's a second reason this matters beyond your own protection: a lot of homeowners are filing insurance claims or manufacturer warranty claims on units that failed during exactly this kind of extreme heat event, and the adjuster or warranty administrator on the other end wants documentation, not a phone call describing what happened. A technician who hands a homeowner five clear, dated photos at the end of a job — nameplate, before, failure point, after, final reading — is handing them something they can attach directly to a claim form. That's a faster payout for the homeowner and fewer callback disputes for you, because there's no ambiguity about what was found or what was done.

This also protects you against the specific accusation that comes up most during heat-wave surge pricing: a customer claiming they were overcharged for work that wasn't actually necessary. A photo of a genuinely corroded coil or a burned-out capacitor answers that question before it's asked. Contractors who skip documentation during their busiest weeks are the ones who end up spending an evening later fielding a dispute that five extra photos would have closed instantly.

If you're running a crew rather than working solo, build this into your dispatch checklist rather than leaving it to memory under pressure. A simple end-of-job requirement — five photos, uploaded before the next call — takes less time to enforce than it takes to resolve one warranty dispute after the fact.
