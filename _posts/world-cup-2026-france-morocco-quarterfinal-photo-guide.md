**France beat Morocco 2-0 in the World Cup quarterfinal on July 9 — Kylian Mbappé scored and assisted after missing a first-half penalty, Ousmane Dembélé added the second — and the expected-goals numbers say the scoreline was generous to Morocco.** France produced 3.04 expected goals to Morocco's 0.14. That's not a close match that France happened to win by two; it's a near-total territorial and chance-creation blowout that a two-goal final score doesn't fully capture, and it's exactly the kind of match where most fans photograph the wrong things.

## Why the Scoreline Undersells What Actually Happened

A 2-0 result reads as competitive on paper — plenty of 2-0 games are nervy, backs-and-forth affairs decided by a moment of quality. This wasn't that. An xG gap this size means France spent the match generating high-quality chance after high-quality chance while Morocco barely threatened France's goal at all. Mbappé's missed penalty is the only reason the margin isn't wider on paper; the underlying dominance was total from early in the match.

That distinction matters for anyone trying to actually document the game, because a genuinely dominant performance and a nervy close win produce two completely different visual stories, even when the final score looks similar.

## Photographing Dominance Instead of Just Waiting for Goals

1. **Shoot the buildup, not just the finish.** In a lopsided match, the interesting story is the sustained pressure — repeated overloads on one flank, a fullback pinned deep for 70 minutes straight. A single goal photo misses that entirely.
2. **Get the goalkeeper under siege, not just the scorer.** Morocco's keeper facing wave after wave of France pressure is a more honest visual summary of a 3.04-to-0.14 match than any single celebration shot.
3. **Capture the away end early, before it empties out.** A one-sided scoreline drains a traveling crowd's energy fast — the rawest reaction shots of a dominant loss usually come in the first half, not after the final whistle.
4. **Don't over-rely on burst mode for the two goals and neglect everything else.** A blowout is 90 minutes of one team's rhythm; treating it like a tight match where you're only poised for two big moments means missing the actual story of control.

## What to Do With the Photo Set Afterward

A dominant win still produces a lot of images if you're shooting the buildup properly — sustained pressure sequences generate more usable frames than a nervy match's handful of genuine chances. Cull hard for the shots that actually show control (an overload, a keeper under duress, a defense pinned back) rather than keeping every frame from 90 minutes of one-sided territory, then compress before sharing.
