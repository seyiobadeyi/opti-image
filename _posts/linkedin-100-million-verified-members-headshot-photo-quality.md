**LinkedIn's verification program just crossed 100 million members, and a new self-serve API means that badge is starting to show up on other platforms too — Zoom is the first to display it directly.** That's a real expansion of what "verified" signals across the professional internet, and it makes an unglamorous problem more visible: a huge number of verified profiles are still using a headshot that undercuts the exact credibility the badge is supposed to add.

## What Actually Changed

Verification confirms that a person is who their profile claims to be — a real identity check, not a quality endorsement. Extending that badge to third-party platforms via API means a verified LinkedIn identity now travels with a user into other professional contexts, starting with Zoom. That's a meaningful trust signal expansion, and it puts more scrutiny on a profile once someone clicks through to check.

## Why a Verified Badge Makes a Bad Headshot More Noticeable

A verified badge draws the eye. It signals "look here, this is confirmed real," and that attention lands directly on whatever photo sits next to it. A blurry, poorly cropped, harshly lit, or years-out-of-date headshot next to a fresh verification badge creates a small but real credibility gap — the platform is vouching for identity while the photo itself is quietly undermining the professional impression that identity is supposed to support.

This matters more now than it did before the badge started appearing outside LinkedIn itself. A verified profile photo on Zoom, in a meeting with people who've never worked with you before, is doing first-impression work in a context with real stakes — much higher scrutiny than a passive LinkedIn feed scroll.

## What an Actual Professional Headshot Requires

1. **Even, front-facing lighting.** Natural window light works better than most people expect — position yourself facing the light source, not with it behind you, which is the single most common headshot mistake.
2. **A plain or softly blurred background.** A cluttered home office or a busy public space competes with your face for attention in a small thumbnail-sized crop.
3. **A tight, centered crop on head and shoulders**, roughly square, matching what LinkedIn actually displays rather than a full-body photo cropped awkwardly by the platform.
4. **Enough source resolution to hold up at both sizes** LinkedIn shows it — the tiny comment-thread avatar and the larger profile view — without looking soft or pixelated at either.
5. **A photo taken within the last year or two.** A headshot from a job five roles ago creates its own small credibility gap once someone meets you in person or on a video call.

## Fixing It Takes Less Time Than People Assume

Most people avoid updating a LinkedIn headshot because it feels like it requires a professional photographer and a scheduled shoot. It doesn't — a phone, a window, and five minutes produces a perfectly usable result for most roles. The step people actually skip is the export: a phone photo straight off the camera roll is often 4-8MB, far larger than needed, and not cropped to the square format the platform expects.
